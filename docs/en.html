The Mathematical Shape of the Architecture — Part A: The Shape

About this edition

This paper posits that the world it deals with has more than one contexture. It does not prove this: its theorems hold without the posit; only their reading as limits between contextures depends on it. Günther named the opposite position. That the universe we live in is mono-contextural he calls "a dogma proven by nothing, which admittedly follows necessarily from classical ontology" (our translation) (source-verified, tml 1971 p. 8, on the page image). In the same sentence he concedes what an opponent would rightly say: from classical ontology the single contexture follows necessarily. What is disputed is only that it is proven. The opposite position therefore rests on a posit as well, and neither of the two is the neutral default. This paper names its posit first; of a reader who disagrees it asks only that he name his in the same way.

This paper has four parts. Part A shows the shape of the architecture in words, by Günther's two plans. Where a theorem of the corpus carries a statement, its name stands in code type and its number in square brackets; a reader who skips both loses nothing of the course of the text. The few computations Part A shows are elementary. The report says what of it has become operational, separated by provenance and in the form the preface describes. Part B is the apparatus, with the appendix to the report. Part C gathers the measured limits. Whoever wants to know whether anything here is overstated will find in Part B, for every number, the theorem, its axiom profile and its range, together with the counting routes, the apparatus of checks and the list of errors this project has found in itself. The abbreviations of the sources, such as HKN or IGN, are resolved by Part B at its beginning.

The separation has a reason. In the first three editions the bookkeeping stood beside every result, and the reader heard the caveats louder than the theorems. Here the matter stands; the evidence stands together in one place.

The numerals in square brackets point to the carrier table in Part B. Where the text says proved, a machine-checked theorem stands there. Where it says posited, an explicit premise stands there. Where it says interpretation, nothing stands there — interpretation is the assignment of the formal to the conceptual, and it is called what it is.

They are edition-internal. Like footnote numbers they are assigned afresh in each edition; whoever wants to refer to a theorem names it from the table, not by its numeral.

Interpretation takes three shapes, and one test separates them: how many passages of the source carry the statement, and does one carry it alone?

  • Assignment — a term of Günther's is attached to something formal.
  • Reading — the paper says about exactly one passage something it does not say in these words.
  • Synopsis — two or more passages carry the statement together, and none carries it alone.

Where a place names its shape, the shape is determined; where it says only interpretation, the shape is not determined, and the absence of the note means nothing.

What this edition changes compared with the ninth

The ninth edition remains reachable and is not updated. The tenth changes it in four ways.

It has a new main part. Between Part A and Part B there stands a report, "What has become operational". It tells, by subject, in seven sections, what of Günther's and Kaehr's constructions stands here proved, executable and checkable: the coupling in Mahler's model, with Kaehr's distinction; Pfalzgraf's fibering; Kaehr's separating case and his calculus, Günther's implications as transjunctions, a bridge between two layers of the corpus, and the small figure with which Günther speaks three times. It ends with Günther's question, not with our answer.

It speaks differently. In the report the provenance of every statement stands in words in the sentence: "Günther writes", "after Mahler", "we stipulate", "proved". Marks in brackets, as Part A carries them, do not occur there; the names of the theorems stand in the appendix. Where "proved" stands, a number stands, and the appendix resolves it. Where a quotation is checked on the page image, the sentence says so, and whether by one hand or by two; where only the text layer of a file has been read, it says that. Every silence names its reach: over which writings, which pages.

It corrects. What the ninth edition said wrongly, too strongly or too weakly stands in the list of errors in Part B, each with the period during which it was delivered. One entry is an understatement of the source; a list that corrects only overstatements sees half.

It says who checks. The voices that worked on this text are roles of instances of the same model family. They have found many errors in one another, and the list of errors shows it. Independent checkers they are not. Independent checks come from the proof environment, for the theorems, and from the author of the project. Whoever distrusts the marks, readings and quotations of this text is right to do so until a voice from outside has read them; a place for that is not yet set up.

Part A is touched where the report requires it: the paragraph on the full cycle, a sentence on Table IX, a paragraph on the connecting link, a sentence on the genesis, the readings on I and Thou, cognition and volition, the proemial relation and the relation of rank, two paragraphs on Lille and the intervals, one detail in the dedication to Mahler, and, in the block on the proemial relation in chapter 7, a page for a quotation and three references written out. Part B has the appendix to the report, a paragraph on the rules and one on the reading file. Part C has a new paragraph in C.1 and two new sections at the end: what the report leaves open, and Günther's own judgement on the full cycles, by which the word "operational" is to be measured.

The shape in one sentence. A combinatorics with a built-in direction carries logics whose separation does not consist in a missing map between them; Günther's two plans order what is built on it, and full cycles through his negation system run through the whole compound while every single step stays within one logic; this direction reappears as time and as ascent; the distance between growing richness and unchanging reachability is its measure; it holds exactly as far as order reaches — the same order that the exchange of two values enforces without ever grounding it; and where cognition and volition are read as its two strands, their connection is thereby regulated and not grounded.

For Thomas Mahler

This paper stands in a line it did not itself found. Thomas Mahler's Morphogrammatik (Klagenfurt 1993, written in Bochum and Dortmund) worked kenogrammatics out as a formal theory where Günther had left it as a programme — keno-sequences, normal forms, concatenation, degree of accretion. It arose in the research project Theorie komplexer biologischer Systeme under the direction of Rudolf Kaehr, who continued Günther's work after his death; that Kaehr took his doctorate with Günther is said concordantly by several sources, and this paper reports it as their information and not as its own.

What is formalised here would not have been possible in this form without that earlier work. We have read Mahler's concatenation and built it differently where our tool allowed — functionally rather than over a list of triples —; that is no objection to it but its use. Where this paper gets further, it gets further on paths not its own, and where it does not get further, it stands at the same limits Mahler himself named.

On the bibliographic record: the version used here names Thomas Mahler alone on its title page. Some catalogues list the printed edition of 1993 under two names, Kaehr and Mahler; the printed edition was not available for inspection.

Two plans

This edition orders its theorems by two plans, and both are Günther's.

The first is the eightfold thematic of objective spirit (Lille 1968). Günther obtains its intervals from an "ascending scale of designations by one value, by two values, by three values and so on" and adds up the values these designations take in (our translation) (source-verified, Lille p. 7). The length of an interval is the number of themes treated in it (source-verified, Lille p. 11). The intervals begin at 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, 28, 36.

The second are the compound contextures, the joining of several two-valued logics. The first is the three-valued system. The next higher ones, Günther writes, "are indicated by the value numbers 6, 10, 15, 21, 28, …", computable by the formula m(m−1)/2 (our translation) (source-verified, HKN p. 26, on the page image).

The two sequences coincide from the second term on. What forces this is not depth but an identity: 1 + 2 + … + k = C(k+1, 2) (computed). The equality of the numbers is therefore arithmetic. A finding about Günther's text is something else: that he hangs his two plans on two different constructions, a sum over designation types and a number of pairs, which this identity brings together (source-verified, both constructions). That he connects the two plans with each other is stated in no passage found. The connection is ours (synopsis). In the corpus the sequence of compound contextures is defined from the sequence of interval starts (verbundWertzahl), not proven from it.

This is how the order of this paper is built: The order of the theorems is founded by Günther's plans. The connection of the plans is founded by this house. No theorem connects the theorems.

The build has grown since the previous edition, but like a fan, not like a tower. Only few load-bearing theorems are consumed outside their module, among them exactly_fortyfour, inv_is_min_length and three_iff_adj (measured at single places, not across the whole corpus). Each load-bearing theorem is textbook material by itself. Together they are a calibration: Günther's combinatorics stands under formal proof, and it holds. Beside them stand findings that are not calibration: where Günther grounds his plan and where not, the two constructions behind his two plans, the identification of his two mediations, a limit whose place is now determined, and a finding of this build which the previous edition had attributed to Günther. They stand one by one, each with its mark.

The eightfold thematic

In the Structural Minimal Conditions of a Theory of Objective Spirit, Günther divides objective spirit into eight themes: a being without reflection that recurs at every stage, three themes of nature, three of subjective spirit, and an eighth that arises from the exhaustion of reflection (source-verified, Lille p. 19).

In the corpus each of the eight places has a correlate verified at the term; open is only the passage beyond the eighth place, called AT-1b here (proved [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], per correlate). Which formalization is which place is our attribution, not a statement of Günther's (assignment). Among the eight correlates only the eighth, the reaching of a fixed point, carries the axiom of choice; the proof that excludes return, at the third place, uses no axiom at all (measured, profiles in Part B). At three points the formalization says something about Günther's text.

Where he grounds his plan and where not. The division into one and seven Günther grounds himself: only one value is positive, the others are its reflections (source-verified, Lille). The division of the seven into three and three he gives in Lille without a reason (source-verified, range: Lille). The build separates the two triads at a change of carrier. The themes of nature are properties of a process; on the lifted carrier of its reflection there then stand exchange, mediation and ground, and freedom from return does not lift along (reflect_not_noreturn) (proved [9]). That is a decision of the build, not a reason Günther gave (posited).

One process, two names, two forms. Of the eighth place Günther says that subjectivity returns into its ground and that with it the next interval begins (source-verified, Lille). In the build the two versions exclude each other. That follows from the choice to bind one form to a fixed point; both statements about the eighth place are projections of the same definition (exhausts_ground, decomp_never_exhausts) (proved [10], [11], definitional). What remains as substance is a theorem: the step across the intervals always goes on and has no fixed point (dstep_no_fixpoint) (proved [12]). The second form, the beginning of the next interval, rests on the decomposition of every value number into interval start and remainder (decomp, dstep_decomp, decomp_never_exhausts) (proved [13], [11]). This decomposition does not redeem the passage AT-1b. Whether Günther's process is thereby two, or one with two sides, the build does not decide (reading).

The upper edge. What lies beyond the eighth place is in Part C.

At the one, the two new chapters touch. The being without reflection has a correlate in the corpus, the fixed point that recurs at every stage (RecurringGround) (proved [1]). It is not an elementary contexture in the sense of the corpus: under that name the corpus carries only pairs of values. Günther's self-cycle, the one-valued form of the elementary contexture, is deliberately not built. The next chapter returns to this.

A point Günther excludes himself. That the even perfect numbers fall on interval starts is his own conjecture. He arrived at it "from considerations of reflection theory" and communicated it to Heinz von Foerster, who checked it (our translation) (source-verified, Lille pp. 13 f.). Mathematically it is the theorem of Euclid and Euler (textbook). Günther treats the matter as an excursus and records: "For the problem of the structural minimal conditions of a theory of objective spirit, the considerations of this excursus have not been taken into account." (our translation) (source-verified, Lille) This paper follows him in that.

How the fourth and the fifth value enter, Günther shows in the Strukturelle Minimalbedingungen einer Theorie des objektiven Geistes ("Lille") at the tables: "We add a fourth value to this three-valued systematics, and the question is: will this value now likewise have the character of designation or not? The question is easy to answer." (our translation) (Lille p. 5, checked on the page image). And of the fifth: "It reflects and repeats the two-valued designation of the three- and of the four-valued system. But with that the one-valued designation is automatically deprived of its non-designative counter-value." (our translation) (p. 6, checked on the page image). That an order of the roles lies in this, the mirrored before the mirroring, and that its criterion is the genesis, we read from these pages; the words are ours. Günther's Tables I to III are calibrated in the corpus [101].

For the intervals that Lille gives the value numbers, the corpus holds: two and five values exhaust their interval, three and four values do not [102]. That is a theorem about the construction, not a statement of Günther's.

The compound and its cycles

Günther says in one sentence what a compound contexture is: "a repeated, relatively independent occurrence of classical logic at different ontological 'world-places'" (our translation) (source-verified, tml 1971).

Two stipulations and a table

Günther posits the ground of this chapter himself, and he says that he posits. "We stipulate further that a negation system, considered calculus-theoretically, represents nothing other than the totality of all permutations of which a given value number n is capable." (our translation) (source-verified, tml 1971 p. 10, on the page image) And one page later: "If we now stipulate that an elementary contexture is constituted by a symmetrical exchange relation of any two values, then the simplest form of a compound contexture consists of three elementary contextures." (our translation) (source-verified, tml 1971) The "further" of the first passage points to an earlier stipulation not read here.

Between the two stands a statement: Table II, which Günther gives for three values, "represents the negation structure underlying the simplest case of a compound contexture" (our translation) (source-verified, tml 1971 p. 10, on the page image). Its last column carries no equals sign. It carries a single operator sign, with 2.1.2 above, 1.2.1 below, and below that the values 3 · 2 · 1 (source-verified, on the page image). Günther writes two paths one above the other that lead to the same place.

The two shortest paths from the initial arrangement into the reversal 3 2 1 are exactly N₁N₂N₁ and N₂N₁N₂ (genesen_kuerzeste) (proved [14]). That they arrive at the same place is the braid relation. The notation of the table asserts this equality without needing a sign for it. As an equation it appears in 1962, as a definition (cyb p. 34) (source-verified).

The elementary contexture — three ranges

The word has three ranges in this paper, and they are kept apart:

  • In Günther an elementary contexture is a self-cycle, one value onto itself, or a two-cycle, the exchange of two values (source-verified, HKN). His sentence fits this: "to describe an elementary contexture we need two values; a one-valued ontology therefore reflects itself in a two-valued logic" (our translation) (source-verified, tml 1971 pp. 10 f.).
  • In the corpus every pair of values is called an elementary contexture (IsElemContexture, a two-element set). Two families are named: the neighbouring pairs {i, i+1} and the mediated pair {i, i+2} (proved [15], [16]). The self-cycle is not built.
  • In the paper of 1971 the non-neighbouring pair 1⟷3 is expressly counted among the elementary contextures (source-verified, tml 1971).

The mediated elementary contexture

Why non-neighbouring values do not exchange directly, Günther says himself: the values are "direction-oriented ordinal numbers", and P "is neither the immediate predecessor nor the immediate successor of N 2" (our translation) (source-verified, IGN pp. 17 f.). The exchange of non-neighbouring values therefore goes through the shared value, and Günther ties this mediation to the second negation (source-verified, IGN p. 19).

In the corpus this is built. The pair {i, i+2} is an elementary contexture (isElemContexture_Em), and the mediated exchange swaps exactly this pair, for every value number (mediated_elem) (proved [16], [17]).

The paper of 1971 identifies two mediations that stand apart elsewhere. To the exchange relations 1⟷2 and 2⟷3 is added "a 'mediated' exchange relation of the values 1⟷3", and this is one of the three elementary contextures of which the simplest compound contexture consists (our translation) (source-verified, tml 1971). What IGN calls the mediated is here a constituent of the compound.

How the elementary contextures touch

Two negators whose pairs of values share a value generate the cycle relation. Two negators whose pairs share no value commute. Both hold for every value number (overlap_iff, disjoint_iff_comm, overlap_iff_three) (proved [18], [19], [20]). The negative half is in Günther: non-neighbouring negators stand "side by side without connection" (our translation) (source-verified, IGN p. 48). That the positive half is his statement is attribution (assignment).

For four values the negation system is a solid: the 24 arrangements are the vertices, the negators the edges, and the graph is the truncated octahedron (computed). Its hexagons are the faces of two negators that share a value, its squares the faces of N₁ and N₃. The square at the start 1 2 3 4 is the path that Günther gives in Table IX in both directions and calls a pseudo-circle (IGN p. 41; the report, "The small figure"). That the table shows this square is our assignment. The cycle relation at the overlap and the commuting at the separation are the division of this surface into hexagons and squares (textbook).

Joining and compound contexture

Günther reads the same sequence 3, 6, 10, … twice: as the value numbers of his compound contextures and, two paragraphs later, as the number of elementary contextures "that must come together in each case" (our translation) (source-verified, HKN p. 26, on the page image). Under the second reading his compound contexture coincides with the joining of all pairs of values exactly once, at three values. From six values on the joining takes more pairs than Günther's compound has elementary contextures: 15 against 6 (verbund_eq_pairs_iff) (proved [21]).

The paper therefore keeps two words apart. Joining is the built notion, which exists for every value number from three on. Compound contexture is Günther's notion, which has a stage only at his value numbers 3, 6, 10, … Four values are no such stage. Günther's cycles on four values, discussed next, lie between two stages of his compound contextures.

The cycles

A full cycle is a sequence of negations that runs through every arrangement of the values exactly once and returns to the start. The word is Günther's. He calls such a circle circle and Hamilton circle, for three values (IGN pp. 18 f.; HWN p. 20) as for four (IGN p. 41), and he calls it full cycle, Vollkreis (IGN pp. 49, 57 f.). That his full cycle means the same as that of this construction, every arrangement exactly once, is suggested by a place where he distinguishes it from the circle "at which not all" permutations take part (our translation) (IGN p. 57, checked on the page image by two hands); that is our assignment. The bare word Kreis, circle, Günther also uses for the cycle relation between two stations. Where it matters, this text therefore says full cycle, with Günther's own word.

For every value number. Where Günther speaks of every value number, he posits the negation system and the condition that only neighbours exchange directly (source-verified, tml 1971 p. 10; Die Metamorphose der Zahl p. 7), not the full cycle (range: Part B). Full cycles he shows for three and for four values. The gap between these cases and the general one is closed by a theorem: for every value number there is a full cycle (sjt_full) (proved [22]). The procedure is that of Steinhaus, Johnson and Trotter (textbook: Steinhaus 1958, Trotter 1962, Johnson 1963). English bell-ringers knew it as plain changes long before. The theorem meets Günther's own sequences: for three values his sequence (5) (sjt2_eq), for four values his second circle in the extended version described in the next paragraph, up to the mirroring N₁ ↔ N₃ (sjt3_mirror_kreis2) (proved [23], [24], calibration). Among the 44 cycles, four carry the same distribution of negators as this one, two up to mirroring. The distribution was fixed by Günther's plan to take each of his three circles from a different family (source-verified, IGN p. 43). Within the family he chose, of the two classes, the one that the procedure of Steinhaus, Johnson and Trotter generates (measured on the corpus). The paper claims no reason for this choice.

The second circle and its print. Günther's second circle, as printed in IGN, does not return to the start (kreis2Gedruckt_not_closed) (proved [25]). The page itself demands the missing steps. In the same paragraph Günther counts that "N 1 occurs nine times, the next negation N 2 appears six times" and N 3 operates nine times (our translation). The printed circle has 8, 6 and 8; only the extended one has 9, 6 and 9 (source-verified, IGN p. 44; counted). Extended by two steps, N₁ and N₃, it becomes a full cycle, and no other extension by two steps achieves this (kreis2_emendation) (proved [26]). Günther's own print of 1974 contains the extended sequence, shifted by one step (janus1974) (source-verified, proved [27]).

The 44. For four values Günther gives 44 full cycles (source-verified, IGN). Why he could not determine the number himself he writes in the afterword to Das Janusgesicht der Dialektik: "The reason for this is to be sought in the fact that at the time of writing his treatise the author of the lecture had no access to a computer." Dr. Alexander Andrew in Reading programmed the question, and "according to the machine" there are 88 (our translation) (source-verified, Janusgesicht p. 23). For the 44 Günther thanks Dipl.-Math. Gerhard Thomas (source-verified, IGN, n. 40).

Both numbers are right, and they count the same thing differently. The corpus counts 88 sequences from a fixed start and 44 up to reversal (exactly_fortyfour and the directed list in the same module) (proved [28], without the axiom of choice). The machine of 1974 counted with direction, Thomas without. 44 is the number of Hamiltonian cycles of the truncated octahedron. It stands in Wolfram's graph data (measured: GraphData, a curated database) and has been recomputed on the graph (computed). Here it is formally checked; an exhaustive enumeration is a proof.

The connecting link, counted. Günther writes that N₂ has "more to accomplish as a logical connecting link than the other two operators", and he names an index for it: "An index of this fact is the circumstance that in four-valued full cycles N 2 must occasionally occur twelve times, while the operators N 1 and N 3 are never needed more than ten times, and on the other hand N 2 can never exercise its exchange function fewer than six times, while N 1 and N 3 are occasionally called upon only five times." (our translation) (IGN p. 49, checked on the page image). In the corpus there is an equation for this. A contact is a place in the circle at which N₁ and N₃ follow each other immediately, without the connecting negation between them. Proved: in every four-valued full cycle the number of contacts, increased by twice the number of N₂, equals 24. The equation is calibrated against Günther's three circles, with 6, 12 and 0 contacts [103]. A full cycle is without contact if and only if N₂ occurs twelve times, and these are exactly the circles in which N₁, N₂ and N₃ occur six, twelve and six times, Günther's third family; Günther himself determines the families by the frequency of the operators (IGN p. 43, Table (18), checked on the page image) [104]. That the count is an index of the connecting link, Günther says. The equation is ours, a consequence. The word contact is ours. At the apparent cycle (IX), in which every pair of neighbours is a contact, Günther says: "In reality 2 two-valued systems of N 1 and N 3 stand side by side without connection." (our translation) (p. 48, checked on the page image). That the contact matches this side by side is our assignment. That a single contact in a full cycle is already a place without mediation is our reading.

Günther's claims about these cycles. What Günther says without proof about the four-valued cycles stands here as theorems: that in a full cycle the three operators never repeat equally often, that the cycles fall into families, and that for three values there is only one circle (source-verified, IGN pp. 29, 43; proved, as compilations: no_888_all [29], the family theorem [30], triadic_unique [31]).

From this a sentence about this part of his work can be formed, and it is our synopsis: His claims about the cycles of his negation systems hold, as far as we have checked them. The number he could not give; in 1974 a machine in Reading gave it, in 1979 a colleague counted it; here it is formally checked (synopsis).

The second negation: range, genesis, mediation (IGN pp. 17–19)

The range. The second negation reaches beyond the classical pair. For n values, n! − 2 arrangements lie outside the two that N₁ alone reaches; for three values these are the four columns Günther gives (transklassisch_card) (proved [32]; source-verified, HKN p. 24).

The genesis. The two paths into the reversal, N₁N₂N₁ and N₂N₁N₂, are the two geneses Günther carries from 1962 to 1979. They are the shortest and the only ones of this length (genesen_kuerzeste) (proved [14]); the hexagon figure shows them. IGN p. 19 prints "N 1.2.3" for the forward path of the first permutation group. For three values there is no third negator. Three lines later, in the same paragraph, the page prints "N 1.2.1" for the return path of the second group, and its table is symmetrical: the return path of one group takes the negators of the forward path of the other. The page itself therefore demands "N 1.2.1", and the corpus confirms this form (Part B, note on genesen_kuerzeste). Where the paper quotes the passage, it reads [1.2.1]. For every value number the mirroring of the values carries the shortest paths into the reversal into each other (proved [105]). What Günther sees in these two paths, from 1970 to 1980, the report tells in the section "The small figure".

The mediation. The mediated exchange is described above (mediated_swap, mediated_elem). Günther ties it to the second negation (source-verified, IGN p. 19).

What in this is trans-contextural. Every negator stays within its range. The operations of a contexture, Günther writes, "remain strictly enclosed in the respective contextural domain in which they arose. Subjective thought cannot propagate itself into the object. The belief in this is magic." (our translation) (source-verified, tml 1971 p. 9, on the page image) A full cycle consists only of such steps and still runs through all arrangements. The trans-contextural arises here not through a foreign operation but through the chaining of enclosed ones; it lies in the order of the steps, not in the step (reading). The single step that would leave the contexture does not exist in the corpus; Part C records this as a definitional gap. The order of the steps exists.

Günther's time as a path. It is on exactly these sequences that Günther reads time: "Time plays a role in this insofar as the negation sequences N 1·2·1·2·1·2 and N 2·1·2·1·2·1 are always a path of reflection that runs ahead in time and that, through the different order of the negations, can take two shapes, which in turn produce an ambiguity." (our translation) (source-verified, IGN pp. 19 f.) Elsewhere he calls time "the ontic shape of the exchange relation" (source-verified, IGN), and he gives the reason why the genesis counts and not only its result: "mediation is essentially a problem of the combination of time and timeless logic" (our translation) (source-verified, HKN). The corpus has no theorem about time as a path. This path is something other than the two times without a common measure that Chapter 3 is about.

Günther names as the connection of number and will "what can be done, or what ought to be done" (our translation) (source-verified, IGN p. 54). The can is built here: the negators, the second negation, the full cycles (assignment).

1 · The ground: a combinatorics prior to logic

At the very bottom lies something that is not yet a logic. One may picture it as a row of empty places into which nothing has yet been entered. Two such rows count as the same if the same places repeat one another — nothing further is asked. No values, no truth, no negation; only distinguishability and repetition.

That sounds poorer than it is. Take the row

a  b  b  a  c

and beside it

x  y  y  x  z

They consist of different signs and are nevertheless the same pattern: the first and fourth place carry the same thing, the second and third carry the same thing, the fifth carries something new. What counts here is not what stands at a place, but which places agree.

Three granularities, and why only the finest carries

Günther distinguishes three degrees by which such rows may be classified, and the distinction is not his invention alone but an obvious gradation.

The coarsest counts only how many distinct signs occur: three in both rows above. The middle one records how often something repeats, without regard to position: here two blocks of size two and one of size one. The finest retains the position-exact pattern.

Only the finest can be the ground of a logic, and the reason is structural, not aesthetic. An assignment — the entering of values into the places — must be able to say which places carry the same value. Whoever knows only the block sizes does not know the assignment: "two blocks of size two" fits a b b a as well as a a b b, and those are different assignment situations. The coarser degrees are therefore readable marks of the ground, not possible grounds.

The normal form, and why it is built rather than asserted

The finest pattern can be written canonically. One numbers the signs in the order of their first occurrence: the first gets zero, each new one the next higher number. From a b b a c comes

0  1  1  0  2

and from x y y x z the same. Two rows carry the same pattern if and only if their normal forms agree — that is proved [33], in both directions and in a form that does not depend on the kind of signs [34], [35].

These normal forms are known in combinatorics; what is remarkable is not their existence but that the architecture has built the canonisation as a procedure and not as a claim. There is a prescription that computes the normal form of any row, and a decidable predicate that says whether a given row is already in normal form. The difference matters: an asserted uniqueness can be false, a built procedure computes.

A construction decision stands here too, and it could easily have gone otherwise. Pattern identity could be introduced as a quotient — one simply declares rows with the same pattern to be the same object. The architecture does not do it. A quotient immediately creates connective work and conceals what the normal form leaves visible; as long as the normal-form semantics carries, it is a purchase on credit. This is no embarrassment but a choice, and it stands so in the corpus.

The normal forms of length n correspond exactly to the partitions of an n-element set; both have the same number (KPC.card_rgs_eq_card_finpartition, proved [36]). That this number is the Bell number the theorem does not say.

The built-in direction

Now comes the first piece of information that goes beyond mere ordering.

A pattern can be extended — one place appended — or retracted: the last place struck. These two operations are not mirror images.

Take the pattern 0 1 1 0. It contains two distinct signs. For the fifth place there are three possibilities: it repeats the first sign, it repeats the second, or it introduces a third. In general a pattern whose largest sign carries the number k has exactly k+2 extensions. Retraction, by contrast, is unique: strike the last place, and precisely one pattern remains.

This is no notational nicety but the matter itself: ascent chooses, descent computes. Whoever extends makes a decision that does not follow from what is given; whoever shortens performs an operation whose result is fixed. Between the two directions lies not a change of sign but a difference in the kind of determinacy.

Günther calls two directions evolutive and emanative — terms he takes over from the metaphysics of history and brings to bear on the kenogrammatic layers. That beneath them lies a pure counting asymmetry, k+2 against one, is stated nowhere in his work; it is a finding of this construction (assignment). This asymmetry is the germ of what returns in chapter 3 as time and in chapter 6 as ascent — and that at this point it is nothing but a count is what makes it load-bearing.

Canonisation knows the direction too

The normal form and the striking of a place are compatible — but only at one end. Number an initial segment on its own and the result is the same as numbering the whole and then cutting (proved [37]); the reason is that assignment goes by first occurrence, and an initial segment has the same first occurrences.

At the other end this fails. Cut at the front and the numbering starts over: from 0,1 striking the first place gives the row 1, and that is no normal form — canonised it reads 0. A sign that was the second is now the first.

The direction thus appears a second time in this chapter, and at a place where one does not expect it: not in the relation of the patterns to one another, but in the prescription that makes them comparable at all. Canonisation is compatible with shortening from behind and not with shortening from the front. What is called the descent in chapter 6 has its ground here.

Where the ground and the logic hang together

So far there has been no talk of values. The transition to logic is the moment when values are entered into the places. And here it emerges that the ground does not simply accept the range of values.

The question is: which patterns can be filled with how many values at all? It has a sharp answer. A pattern can be filled with two values if and only if its signs do not go beyond one — proved [38].

One direction is an encoding: whoever has only zeros and ones in the pattern writes down the two values. The other direction is the interesting one. If a two occurs in the pattern, then — because of the construction of the normal form, in which each new sign exceeds the previous maximum by exactly one — there are three places with pairwise distinct signs. Three pairwise distinct signs require three pairwise distinct values, and a two-valued row does not have them. The closing argument is a counting argument, and it is the one place where the proof goes beyond mere recoding.

Worked through at a fixed length, the matter becomes tangible. At four places there are fifteen distinct patterns; eight of them can be filled with two values — and both numbers are proved [39], [40] and not looked up. The unequal pair 15 to 8 says: the ground is richer than any fixed valency. A witness of this excess is quickly found — the pattern

0  1  2  0

requires three values, and no two-valued row satisfies it.

Whoever recalls classical logic will recognise the number: the sixteen binary truth functions rest on eight patterns. The other seven do not occur in it — not because they were overlooked, but because two values do not reach them.

Why eight patterns carry sixteen tables

Up to here that was an observation: two numbers that fit together. It has become a calculation, and the calculation has three parts.

The fibre. To every two-valued fillable pattern of length four there belong exactly two truth tables, and which of the two is at hand is decided by the filling of the first place alone [41]. The theorem is not built as a count, but as a correspondence to the two truth values themselves: the first place chooses, everything else follows from the pattern. The two tables over one pattern come apart by a global exchange of values — they agree at no place whatever. Determined by the first place does not mean different only at the first place; it is the first that decides, and after that everything flips with it.

The calculation. With this, 16 = 2 · 8 is no longer an observation but an equation between two figures of this corpus [42]: on the left the truth tables over four rows, on the right the two-valued fillable patterns. It is Günther's own calculation, and it now stands on both sides within the corpus rather than on one.

The pair of witnesses. The excess above can be graded instead of merely asserted. The pattern 0 1 2 0 requires three values and not two; the pattern 0 1 2 3 requires four and not three [43]. The steps two, three and four are thereby separated at witnesses — and the first of the two witnesses is precisely the pattern that had to carry the excess a few lines above.

What the three theorems do not say. They stand for length four. The general series — two to the power n minus one tables for every pattern of length n — is not built, and this paper does not assert it. And the numbers under which Günther lists his tables are his count; their assignment to the classes built here remains interpretation.

What this chapter does not say

The ground is not a logic, and it does not become one. It carries no truth values, no negation, no inference; all of that comes only with an assignment.

Kenogram is not contexture. When the next chapter speaks of contextures and this one of k-valued fillability, the reading that a k-fillable row is "a k-valued system" is an interpretation — it hangs on the bridge [38] that supports it, and it is expressly labelled as interpretation in the corpus. The theorem says something about values in places; that values in places yield a logic, it does not say.

The threefold gradation of granularities is attested in Günther with this explicitness; the finer division of kinds of map with which the architecture works is not — it is a refinement of this project and does not claim him as its authority.

2 · The contextures: separated, without any map being missing

A contexture is an assignment on the ground that has the rank of a logic of its own. Not a region within an encompassing logic, but a logic: with its own distinction of true and false, self-contained. The overall picture is therefore not one logic with regions, but a web of logics over a shared pre-logical ground.

The question around which everything turns is: wherein does their separation consist?

The case at which the obvious answer fails

The obvious answer would be: two contextures are separated if no translation exists between them — no structure-preserving map.

This answer fails at Günther's own basic case. The most elementary case of discontexturality is for him the opposition of Being and Nothing. And these two are for him isomorphic. He appeals to mathematical logic, in which the matter has long been known "as the isomorphism of two-valuedness and the semantic symmetry of affirmation and negation", and to the mathematician Reinhold Baer, who pointed to this isomorphism at a Hegel congress in 1931. Baer's statement that between a statement and its negation there is "no essential difference" Günther calls "unassailable", and yet he holds that "an essential ontological difference" exists (our translation, from the German column; source-verified, Die historische Kategorie des Neuen p. 10, on the page image: Hermeneutes, gg_category.pdf). Between them there is a complete, structure-preserving map — and they are discontextural nonetheless.

One may read this finding as a contradiction or as a hint. The architecture reads it as a hint, and the hint is precise: the map exists, but it is stated from outside, not executed from within. Whoever stands in one of the two contextures and has only the means that hold there does not arrive at it. This is Günther's own distinction, the same one with which he handles the excluded middle: it holds for both sides and is nevertheless applicable only to one.

The formalisation decision that follows

Discontexturality therefore appears not as the non-existence of a map, but as non-assemblability from the means available inside.

Concretely: one fixes the operations that hold within a contexture — in the simplest case two connectives and a reversal — and asks which operations can be built from them by composition, at any depth and in any wiring. Everything so buildable is inside. What cannot be so built is not.

What is missing is not the map, but the path to it from within.

Two shorter formulations are expressly excluded and carried in the corpus as construction prohibitions. One would be: two contextures are separated if they have nothing in common. The other: they are separated if no map exists between them. The second fails at Being and Nothing, as just shown. The first fails at a fact one can count.

The overlap is counted, not agreed

Consider three values and the two-element regions within them: {0,1}, {1,2}, {0,2}. Any two of them have exactly one element in common. This is no convention that could have been settled otherwise, but a counting statement — and it is proved [45].

That is more than it looks. Were the overlap merely a construction decision, one could drop it and model contextures as disjoint; the prohibition would then be a house rule. Since it is a counted property of the situation, the prohibition is not a rule but a consequence: whoever posits the elementary contextures as disjoint is no longer speaking about the same objects. And the overlap really does carry weight in the proofs — it is the reason why an operation can behave differently on different elementary contextures without leaving any region anywhere. Without the shared value that case would not exist.

The joining: what several contextures are together

Besides the elementary contexture Günther knows the compound contexture — several elementary contextures together, and between them a mediation. The joining is built. It has three statements, and the first of them was a justification in Günther.

At least three values, and now as a theorem. Günther says a compound contexture requires at least three values, and he justifies it by saying that with two the second value stands over against the first unmediated. The corresponding theorem says it without appeal to intuition: more than one elementary contexture — that is, anything at all that could come together — exists exactly from three values on [46]. At two values the situation is not poor but single: there is one contexture, and with one alone nothing joins.

They hang together. Take two distinct elementary contextures: either they overlap, or there is a third that touches both [47]. The connection therefore does not fall apart as soon as the stock of values grows; it only becomes indirect. This is the version that survives the preceding section: pairwise overlap holds at three values, connectedness holds beyond that.

And from four on they also occur without a common value. Two elementary contextures with no element in common exist exactly from four values on [48]. Below that every one touches every other; from four on there are pairs that no longer see each other. It is the same number at which reachability tips over in chapter 5 — what becomes of it there stands there.

What does not stand here is the mediation. The joining is the one piece of Günther's concept; the other is the mediation between the contextures so joined, and in him that hangs on the second negation. No theorem of this paper carries it. Chapter 7 says what exactly that means.

Where the posit sits, and why its ground is firm

One point must be stated openly, because the whole series that follows rests on it.

Every two-element set of values is an elementary contexture in Günther's sense — any two, not only those neighbouring in size. — This is posited, not proved.

No theorem decides whether Günther's concept means precisely this. The posit is the load-bearing choice of the architecture, and it stands as such in the corpus.

But its ground is not arbitrary, and that can be shown. A two-element set is closed under the local operations if and only if its two elements are comparable. If they are not, then even the minimum leaves the set — proved [49]. Then it is no self-contained connection and hence no contexture in the sense of the definition from which the architecture proceeds.

The posit remains a posit. But it does not stand on taste, it stands on a theorem: the limit of the formalisation lies in the concept, not in the proof.

What this chapter does not say

The second fundamental relation is only half redeemed. Beside the symmetric exchange relation between elementary contextures Günther names a second, asymmetric one — that between a contexture and what goes beyond it. Its structural part is available as a theorem; its conceptual part is not. That a change of stage is a change of contexture is closed by no theorem. A contextural boundary never closes by a theorem alone, but by definition and theorem together — and the definition is a posit.

The separation is grasped as non-assemblability and not as an ontological rupture. What is proved are failures of reduction. To read them so that a rupture of being appears in them is interpretation.

3 · Time: a structure that fits into no linear measure

Before the architecture speaks of time, the place where Günther first came upon the matter is worth a look. It lies early, in 1937, and it is agreeably tangible.

Three propositions, each plausible on its own

First: time is more powerful than will. Every decision falls within time. No one can will that a decision had fallen earlier; the will finds the moment at which it expresses itself already given.

Second: thought is more powerful than time. Memory runs counter to time and reaches back to the beginning. Thought is not bound to the direction of the flow — it can survey it, and whoever surveys something is in that respect above it.

Third: will is more powerful than thought. With Fichte: that I think rests on the fact that I will to think. Thought does not set itself in motion.

Take the first two together: if thought is more powerful than time and time more powerful than will, then thought is more powerful than will. The third proposition says the opposite. The three cannot be brought into a ranking; they close into a circle.

Günther draws from this no contradiction but a finding: from such propositions no groups of statements of transitive character can ever be formed, and therefore the course of time in actual consciousness remains always ambiguous.

What the architecture makes of it — and what not

It does not claim that thought, will and time behave this way. That assignment of three names to three corners is interpretation and is carried as such.

What is proved is the other, the hard half: in every transitive, irreflexive ordering — on every carrier, without exception — such a three-cycle is impossible [50]. And there is likewise no structure-preserving image of such a cycle into an ordering [51]; it cannot be accommodated by clever translation either. The core of these theorems manages without the classical instruments of set theory.

The claim is therefore: if the three behave this way, then no linear order is their measure — and not because none has been found so far, but because none can exist. That is less than a philosophy of time promises, and more than an observation achieves.

The time of 1937 as a theorem

Günther's early finding about time has a theorem since this edition. In Wahrheit, Wirklichkeit und Zeit he records that for two times there is "no common, objective (objectual) measure of time" (our translation) (source-verified, WWZ p. 3). In the corpus it follows: two times that reverse each other have no common measure (no_common_measure_inverse) (proved [52], corollary). That Günther's two times reverse each other the paper reads from his text; he does not say it in these words (reading).

This time is not that of the compound chapter. There one path runs ahead in time; here stand two times without a common measure. The paper keeps the two apart.

The stock of movement

Over the web of contextures lies a threefold doctrine of movement — three ways in which something can change, without its yet being said what changes:

  • displacement — the same structure at another place;
  • reorganisation — the same parts in another arrangement;
  • irreversible positing — the step that is not taken back.

Their compositions are smooth in exactly one direction; there is no canonical interchange of directions, and that is not an image but proved [53].

The third mode of movement — irreversible positing, the step that is not taken back — is irreversible for a reason the source names. In a heterarchical circular order every beginning is an arbitrary choice, and once one is chosen there is "keinen originären Grund … zu entscheiden, ob der Kreis links- oder rechtsläufig durchlaufen werden soll" — no original ground for deciding whether the circle is to be run through leftward or rightward; there Günther comes upon the logical motif of contingency, and there "hakt der Wille ein, der zwar immer nur sich selbst, aber sich selbst in der negativen Kontingenz einer noch nicht durchgeführten Empirie sucht" — the will hooks in, which always seeks only itself, but itself in the negative contingency of an experience not yet carried out. Where no ground decides the direction, no computing back is possible: that is the reason for the irreversibility, and it is the same reason for which the building direction of chapter 1 — the one that chooses — chooses at all.

Here Günther stands in his own words, because no theorem hangs on the place: a programme claims nothing about the object. The three determinations — "Willkürwahl", "negative Kontingenz", "noch nicht durchgeführte Empirie" (arbitrary choice, negative contingency, experience not yet carried out) — are terms and therefore stand in quotation, in the German original, with a rendering of this edition beside them. The place is interpretation, namely assignment of the reason to the form; the form does not hang on it. That the three modes of movement of this chapter are Günther's triad likewise remains assignment from the wording and no measurement (Als Wille, p. 4 of the file used; checked word for word against it).

Two differentials, separated by the number of contextures

Time falls into two statements, and what separates them is a single hard quantity: the number of contextures involved.

Within one contexture the cycle of potency stands against every linear order — that is the finding above.

Between contextures the cumulative movement does not close: under an expressly posited premise — that no state reproduces itself — there is no circle, and the ascent admits no reversing assignment. The premise is declared, not hidden; with a self-reproducing state the claim would fall.

The connection with chapter 1

Both statements are the same asymmetry in another guise. At the ground it appeared as a count — ascent chooses, descent computes — and a second time in canonisation itself, which tolerates shortening from behind and not from the front. Here it appears as the impossibility of reversing a direction or bringing it into a measure. In chapter 6 it returns twice: as a provable limit of the ascent and as a coupling that is measured in one direction only.

That is the core of the whole architecture: a single asymmetry — combinatorial, temporal, hierarchical — and more than once in each of the three languages.

Buridan

Günther himself shows what the proemial relation is good for, with Buridan's ass (source-verified, C&V pp. 16 f.). An animal that has to eat stands in an environment that gives it no directive: "the environment does not provide the volitive system with a directive situation from which the will can take its orders" (source-verified, C&V p. 16). What is missing is the directive to the system, not a ranking among the objects. The judgments about the environment, Günther writes, "may motivate the volition but they do not control the mechanism of volitive action" (source-verified, C&V p. 17). Whoever stands in front of six unequal turnstiles at a station is in the same situation: information about the turnstiles is not an order to the traveller (our example). That the animal has to eat belongs to the example; only this compulsion confronts it with the alternative at all (interpretation).

The situation has three phases. Before, an order holds. Then an exchange occurs that is "absolutely neutral in itself relative to the distinction between subject and object" (source-verified, C&V p. 16). After it, the order holds that the animal founds by its choice. The exchange changes the pole and does not replace it, and the act founds both roles at once (reading). One point Günther leaves open himself: whether the exchange entails the new order ("implies", p. 23) or only sets it off ("triggers", p. 16) (source-verified).

The relation describes the situation in which an act becomes due, and what the act changes in the roles. The act itself it does not describe (interpretation). The example claims no carrier for the proemial relation. Where its limit lies is said in Part C.

What this chapter does not say

The naming of the three corners is interpretation. The statement about the relation of thought, will and time is conditional: it hangs on a premise that this paper does not prove and cannot prove. And the premise in the inter-contextural case — freedom from self-reproducing states — is posited.

4 · How one proves a lack

Everything so far runs into negative statements: something cannot be assembled, cannot be brought into an order, cannot be reached with finitely many values. Such statements have a difficulty of their own, and it is not technical but fundamental.

A system that certifies its own limit from within contradicts itself. Whoever says "with my means X is unreachable" uses precisely those means for the statement. The way out that the architecture takes throughout is called the differential: one does not prove positively within one system, but differentially between two.

The procedure always has the same form. One names a poor class — a precisely specified set of structures — and shows: over all its instances the feature in question is impossible. Beside it one places a rich witness, a concrete structure that carries it.

the hard half the easy half
the feature is impossible — over all instances the feature is exhibited — a construction
all orderings; everything buildable from the local means one concrete structure, built inside the system

The impossibility is the hard half. The construction alone would be the easy one, and passing it off for the whole would be the most comfortable form of self-immunisation.

Three marks keep the procedure honest.

First: the poor model is built inside the system, not asserted in a metalanguage. Whoever speaks about "all orderings" must be able to say what he is speaking about.

Second: the result is conditional, relative to the posited premises. It does not compel the sceptic; it symmetrises him — from the comfortable position this can be reduced to the known to the explicit position I posit the one encompassing logic, which is itself a posit and thereby has no precedence.

Third: what is proved is the form, never the naming. That a contexture-dependent choice of operations is unreachable from local means is a theorem. That this choice is called mediation is interpretation. The corpus keeps to this down to its file names: the file carrying the theorem is named after the formal property and not after its philosophical reading.

The procedure applies to the workshop as well. In the most recent working phase it has been applied twice to the architecture itself — once to a theorem about the ascent, whose impossibility half runs over all maps and whose possibility half exhibits an explicit witness (chapter 6), and once to a measurement: where an explanation for a result is given, it requires two versions differing in exactly one quantity. Whoever wants to read that up will find it in Part B.

5 · The measure of the separation

Chapter 2 said wherein the separation consists: in non-assemblability from the means available inside. This chapter says how much that is. For an impossibility becomes information only when one knows how large the unreachable is against the reachable.

The frame is everywhere the same. We have finitely many values ordered by size. The elementary contextures are the two-element regions — any two values, and with four levels that is six pairs, in general m(m−1)/2. The local means are two connectives — minimum and maximum — and the reversal of order. An operation is called locally classical if on every elementary contexture it acts like one of the two connectives and does not leave it. The question is which of the locally classical operations can be built from the local means.

Two values: no excess

At two values there is exactly one elementary contexture and exactly two locally classical operations — minimum and maximum themselves. Both are trivially buildable. The excess is zero, and that is proved [54] and not merely observed.

This meets Günther's own threshold, and does so literally: for him the classical two-valued system does not yet represent a compound contexture; the second value yields nothing new and stands unmediated over against the first. What is a thesis in him stands here as a counted zero. And his justification is by now itself a theorem: at two values there is no second elementary contexture, and there is one exactly from three on [46].

Three values: half, and in steps

At three values there are three elementary contextures, and a locally classical operation is nothing other than the specification of on which of them it acts as minimum and on which as maximum: eight choice patterns. Of these exactly four are buildable and exactly four are not — proved [55].

The four reachable ones are not four arbitrary ones. Ordering the elementary contextures by size, they read

(min, min, min)   (max, min, min)   (max, max, min)   (max, max, max)

— a chain of thresholds. Reachable is exactly whoever switches once from minimum to maximum and then stays. Whoever switches back is outside.

Here the second reason for non-assemblability appears for the first time, and it is the genuinely interesting one. An operation can be unreachable because it leaves its contexture — it delivers at some place a value that does not occur in the two-element region. Or it can be unreachable although it preserves every contexture and acts classically on each: then it is due solely to the fact that it chooses differently on different contextures. The first reason is transcendence, the second is non-uniformity. The second long had no witness; it has one now, and completely so — the division into four and four leaves nothing open.

The first reason has its own witness: an operation that refuses an offered pair of values as a whole and thereby produces the third value. It does not lie in what can be built — proved [56]. And it lies not somewhere outside, but at a determinate place: the local means preserve three properties, and every operation built from them preserves all three — not merely the generators, but every composition. The refusal violates exactly one of them and preserves the other two.

Lying outside is cheap in itself: of the 19,683 binary operations over three values only 82 are buildable, less than half a per cent. The claim does not rest on that. It rests on the location.

The heterarchic pair

Günther exhausts the eight choices of values from conjunction and disjunction in six hierarchical orders of preference and calls the remaining two, KKD and DDK, "heterarchical (or cyclic)" (source-verified, C&V pp. 26–28). Read as tournaments on three values, six of the eight choices are transitive and two cyclic (cyclic_iff) (proved [57]).

The same two appear as early as 1962, and for a different reason. In Cybernetic Ontology Günther singles out two sequences because they "cannot be defined in this simple manner": their definition needs both negators and both bases (source-verified, cyb p. 39). In the corpus these two sequences are exactly the two operations with a non-transitive tournament (tafel_cyclic_pair) (proved [58]). Two different criteria, Günther's definitional one and ours, meet the same set (proved [58], calibration). The transjunction that hangs on these sequences is, in Günther, their consequence, not their reason (source-verified).

The pair does not depend on the convention of the letters K and D: it goes over into itself when all letters are exchanged (cyclic_letter_swap) (proved [59], calibration). It is therefore the same pair as KKD and DDK in C&V p. 27, provided that Günther's order of places in C&V is that of the corpus or its mirror image. Under an arbitrary order of places this does not hold (cyclic_not_swap23) (proved [60]). Which of the two sequences is Günther's KKD depends on the convention; the paper therefore says "the same pair", not "the same sequence".

Günther himself leaves something open here, and he marks it: "It turns out then that the two cyclic negations … do not yield the cyclic functions KKD and DDK. What we obtain instead is KDD and DKD … A highly significant feature which should be noted but which will not be interpreted within the scope of this essay is that … we do not obtain exactly the order of the conjunctive/disjunctive functors which they should have according to their logical strength." (source-verified, C&V p. 27) This paper does not interpret the passage either. Günther refers the notion of logical strength to Carnap; Carnap has not been read here (Part C).

Four values and beyond: two remain

At four values the picture tips. Of the 64 locally classical operations two remain buildable — minimum and maximum themselves [61]. At five values it is 2 of 1,024, at six 2 of 32,768.

And this is no accident of small numbers but a theorem for all value counts from four upwards: a locally classical operation is buildable if and only if it is globally the minimum or globally the maximum [62]. The proof manages without case distinction on the number of values and without machine enumeration; it runs over six families of neighbourhood relations with breaking points given in closed form, and an induction on path lengths.

The threshold lies at four, and it is no artefact. At three values the statement is false — there four of eight are reachable. At two values there is no witness, because there is only one contexture — and that no second one joins it there is the theorem from chapter 2 [46]. Mediation begins at three; it becomes completely unreachable from four upwards.

The same threshold shows itself on the contexture side, and there it is a different statement: only from four values on are there two elementary contextures with no common value [48]. That both thresholds lie at four follows from the two theorems; that they lie there for the same reason is said by neither.

What it means not to be generable

The phrase not constructible has so far always appeared at a witness: an operation that is not, and a proof of it. It also exists as a statement about the whole class.

Whoever is not generable chooses differently. From four values on: if a locally classical operation is not constructible, then there are two distinct pairs, on one of which it acts like the maximum and on the other like the minimum — and the two pairs do not coincide [63]. With this the phrase used several times in this chapter becomes a theorem about the class, and no longer a description of its witnesses.

And its limit belongs beside it. Choosing differently does not mean choosing disjointly. The theorem yields two pairs that are not the same pair; it does not yield two pairs without a common value. The class does not achieve that — a single witness does: over four and more values there is a non-constructible operation and, for it, two elementary contextures with no common value, on one of which it acts like the maximum and on the other like the minimum [64]. Poor class, rich witness — the same shape as with the measure itself, and here for the second time.

It is at the same time the one place where the two threads of this paper hold the same object: the theorem speaks in the terms of chapter 2 — elementary contexture, no common value — about an object of this chapter.

The richness, counted

Where do the numbers 8, 64, 1,024, 32,768 come from? They are not separate counts but instances of one theorem.

A locally classical operation is nothing other than one choice per elementary contexture. That is proved as a one-to-one correspondence [65]: forwards one reads off, for each elementary contexture, whether the operation acts there as maximum; backwards one builds the operation from the choice data. Their number is thereby

2^C(m,2)

for every value count m — proved and not observed. The exponent is the number of elementary contextures, that is, the number of pairs from m values. It is the same number series that Günther carries for the compound contextures and for the gradation of ontologies — here it stands as the exponent of a space of possibilities, and it stands there not as an observation but as a theorem.

"Locally classical" is thereby no longer a mere property but a blueprint: one choice per elementary contexture, nothing further. And the boundary cases carry without side conditions: with at most one value there is no pair and exactly one operation.

The measure

Now the question of this chapter can be answered. From value stage to value stage

  • the richness grows strictly — the number of locally classical operations increases at every step [66], and from three values on it exceeds two [67];
  • the reachable stays at two — from four values on it is exactly minimum and maximum, and at every stage the same two [62].

That is the most precise version of what "mediation needs more than local means" says: a distance between two numbers, one of which grows exponentially and the other of which stays constantly two. Not an existence claim — "there is something unreachable" — but a characterisation: the set of the reachable is completely determined, and everything else lies outside.

How much each bound claims

An impossibility statement hangs on the property it works with. If constant operations are admitted to the local means — checkers that always deliver the same value regardless of input — then the bound survives precisely when its property is also respected by constants.

For the witness of transcendence this is not the case: it falls as soon as constants are permitted. For the characterisation from four values upwards and its applications it is the case: they hold. A bound that falls to an added constant claims less than it appears to — and that is written into the corpus as a duty of inspection before every new witness.

What this chapter does not say

The numbers 82, 19,683 and the pattern counts at five and six values are computed, not proved inside the system; they stand as orders of magnitude and carry no claim. The chain of thresholds at three values is likewise computed.

And: the expressions "locally classical" and "mediation" are not the same thing. The theorem says that a contexture-dependent choice is not buildable from local means. That such a choice is the formal locus of what Günther calls mediation is interpretation — grounded interpretation, but no theorem.

6 · The ascent

Günther takes polycontexturality as a world picture: reality as a system of infinitely expanding contextures with growing structural richness. A world picture is no subject for theorems, and the architecture does not claim it.

But two structural statements are contained in this determination, and those are subjects for theorems. One: it expands, and without end. The other: in doing so the structural richness grows. Both have theorems, and this chapter presents them.

The gap is inhabited everywhere

Chapter 5 says what is reachable. It does not say that the unreachable is also inhabited at every stage — that there is anything there at all that is locally classical and yet not buildable. Now a theorem says it: for every value count from four upwards such an operation exists [68].

The witness is a rule and not a single case: act as maximum exactly on the lowest elementary contexture and as minimum everywhere else. The same prescription at every stage. That the lowest is chosen is a chosen and not a found basis — any other pair would give another witness, and the corpus says so on the spot.

The witness travels along

Now the step from one stage to the next. One embeds the smaller set of values order-faithfully into the larger — the values keep their sequence, and one is added.

On the embedded region the rule of the larger stage agrees with that of the smaller [69]. The witness is thus not lost and need not be chosen anew; it is the same one, seen from further up.

This is the formal version of what Günther's stepwise progression means by "every stage contains the previous one as a substructure" — here as a theorem and not as a programme. Beside it the arithmetic of growth: each step adds exactly m new elementary contextures when going from m to m+1 values [70], and the image of the old stage does not reach the new element [71]. What is added was not there before.

And yet the step does not carry the signature

Here comes the finding no one had reckoned with.

The embedding carries the order, it carries the lattice, it carries the witness. The reversal of order it never carries — at no place, for no value count [72]. The reason is elementary: the reversal reflects the values about the middle, and the middle shifts when a value is added.

One might hope a cleverer embedding heals this. The answer is a differential in the sense of chapter 4, and it comes out hard:

If the value count is odd, then not a single map into the next stage commutes with the reversal of order [73] — without any assumption of injectivity or monotonicity, over all maps whatsoever.

The argument is three lines long and therefore exhibitable. At an odd value count the reversal has a fixed point: the middle value, which is mapped to itself. A compatible map would have to send it to a fixed point of the next stage. The next stage has an even value count — and there the reversal has no fixed point. Hence there is no such map.

And the possibility half stands beside it: at an even value count there is indeed an order-faithful, reversal-compatible embedding — it leaves the gap exactly in the middle [74], [75] — and from four values upwards the witness ascends under it as well [76].

The stage step therefore fails to carry the full local means precisely when the value count is odd. The lattice ascends step by step, the reversal only in jumps of two. Put otherwise: the exchange relation is stage-relative.

What this means is interpretation, and let it be said: the ascent has no form that would close it — the expansion is genuine because it cannot be caught up signature-faithfully. A theorem about the non-existence of a limit object this is not, and none is claimed. About double steps — from m to m+2 — the theorem says nothing, and that too stands so in the corpus.

What the step exchanges — and where it does not

So far it has been said what outlasts the step: the witness yes, the reversal no. It has not yet been said what is interchangeable with it — and that is the sharper question, because it holds the exchange and the ordering step against each other.

For this the chapter returns for a moment to the ground — stage here means length of the row, not number of values.

Take the simplest exchange movement there is on a row: swap two places and bring the result back into normal form. And take the simplest ordering step: strike the last place. Both are total, both are cheap. The question is: does the order of the two matter?

Mostly not. If the swap does not touch the last place, then it is the same whether one swaps first and then strikes or the other way round — the two operations commute (proved [77], for arbitrary rows and without any normal-form assumption).

At the last place it becomes interesting. There interchangeability no longer holds in general — but neither does it simply never hold. It holds exactly under a statable condition, and the condition is a statement about the repetition pattern: the swap changes nothing if the two values agree anyway, or if the place concerned stands alone in the remaining part and the value of the last place does not occur there at all. Otherwise it changes the result of the striking — proved [78], as a biconditional and for normal forms.

Thereby the last place is distinguished not by a naming but by its behaviour: it is the one whose exchange does not commute with the ordering step. That is read off. And that the breaking point lies exactly there is the yield and no incidental: the exchange disturbs the ascent at precisely the place that constitutes the ascent.

The information has a direction, and it belongs to it. What is measured is the exchange against the ordering step — the swapping of two places, measured against the striking of the last. The converse direction, measuring the order against the exchange, occurs in no theorem. What that means for Günther's interlocking stands in chapter 7; it is less than it sounds, and more than what stood there before.

No finite stock of values suffices

The second structural statement — infinitely expanding — is redeemed not by a limit object but by a negative theorem about every finite stock of values.

The fillability bridge of chapter 1 generalises: a pattern can be filled with k values if and only if all its signs lie below k [79], boundary cases included. The way is the same as in the two-valued case, only general: a sign at k or above forces k+1 pairwise distinct values.

Now consider an infinite sequence of patterns whose signs grow beyond every bound. It follows that for every k there is a place from which on the initial segment can no longer be filled with k values [80]. And such a stream exists — the simplest is the one in which every place brings a new sign [81].

That is "infinitely expanding" in its most honest formal shape: not as a claim about an infinite, but as a statement about every finite. The evolutive direction of chapter 1 — the one that chooses — provably exceeds every stock from which it could choose.

The interpretive step is stated expressly: kenogram is not contexture. That a k-fillable row is a "k-valued system" hangs on the bridge that supports it, and is labelled interpretation in the corpus.

The richness grows, the reachable does not

The third ingredient has already fallen in chapter 5 and belongs here: the space of possibilities grows strictly from stage to stage [66], the reachable remains at every stage the same two [62], [67]. That is the quantitative shape of "growing structural richness".

That this gradation is what Günther means is interpretation. What is proved are a correspondence, a cardinality, a set equality and two inequalities.

The concept, second stage

From these four results a concept can be formed that no longer speaks of one structure but of a family of structures. A family is called formally operationally polycontextural in ascent if

  1. every one of its members carries several overlapping contextures on which a locally classical but not buildable operation exists;
  2. the members are joined by embeddings under which this witness is preserved;
  3. the stock of locally classical operations grows strictly from member to member;
  4. at every member exactly the same two named operations remain reachable.

This is satisfied by the stage family of the ordered value sets from four values upwards. Each of the four clauses carries a theorem.

And what is expressly not claimed belongs to the concept and not in a footnote. Not claimed is a preservation of the full local means — the parity obstruction forbids it, which is why the second clause speaks of the witness and not of the signature. Not claimed is a limit object. Not claimed is the world picture.

Deliberately the concept has not been built as an object of its own in the library. A structure with four fields that no theorem requests would be a renaming of what is there — the same decision with which the project once already let a planned naming layer lapse.

What about the world picture

The totality — reality as a system of… — remains open, and is carried as open.

That is a result and not an arrears. A statement of totality is not a structural property; it says nothing about how something is built, but that everything is built so. What is structure in this determination now has theorems. The only formal path to totality would be a constitutive posit — one declares it a premise. That would not approximate the concept, it would posit it, and this path is not taken.

7 · Reach and limit

Where the bound bites

The characterisation of chapter 5 has a face of application, and it is narrower than the name polycontexturality suggests.

It bites where a linear scale of at least four levels is present and a policy is called for that decides classically on every pair of levels — with four levels on all six — but not everywhere alike. Release levels from blocked to privileged; source authority from note to statute; security levels; hierarchies of norms. In such cases the theorem says: a mixed policy is not assemblable from local, level-blind checkers — in whatever wiring and to whatever depth.

And it does not say that the policy is right. From non-assemblability follows no guarantee of security, legality, truth or completeness. What follows is a statement about architecture: at this place a mediating instance of its own must stand, and it cannot be compiled away.

Where the scale is missing, the claim falls. Several roles, several perspectives, several agents — that does not suffice. A transition graph of named roles without gradation is not carried by this bound; what remains provable there is reachability, and a type system achieves that too.

Two ways of falling out of the local means

Up to here one way has been in the foreground: mixture. A policy that decides classically on every pair of levels and is nevertheless not everywhere alike is not assemblable from the local means; its certificate is two pairs on which it acts in opposite ways [63].

The corpus carries a second, independent way: leaving a contexture. A set of values closed under the local means is never left by an assembled expression. If an operation leaves it at a nameable point, it is no such expression [86] — and the certificate names the set and the point.

An example one can check by hand. Over four levels exactly two two-element sets are closed under minimum, maximum and the reversal of order: {0,3} and {1,2} [87]. The rounded-down average of two levels — the familiar rule take the middle — maps 0 and 3 to 1 and thereby leaves {0,3} at the point (0,3) [88]. An auditor thus receives a cell and not a verdict: your rule leaves the contexture {0,3} at (0,3).

Three things that do not follow from this.

The narrowness stated above stands in full. It concerned the first way, and it holds unchanged; the second stands beside it and does not replace it.

The precondition of the scale is not loosened. The second way too computes over the linear scale of levels — its means are minimum, maximum and the reversal of order, and these presuppose the order. Whoever reads a wider reach out of the two ways reads it in: several roles, several perspectives, several agents — that does not suffice holds for both.

And the certificate is sufficient, not necessary. If no point is found at which a rule leaves a closed set, then nothing is shown by that, least of all that the rule were assemblable. There are operations that preserve both sets and are nevertheless not assemblable; for them this paper carries no certificate, and it says so rather than pass over a gap in silence.

The height of the fall, and that it can be stated

What happens if one removes the order? Take the smallest lattice in which three elements stand side by side as equals — a bottom element, three incomparable above it, a top. On it there is an operation that acts classically on every comparable pair and is globally neither the infimum nor the supremum. It is not merely calculated but exhibited as a term and proved [89]:

(x ∧ y) ∨ ((x ∨ y) ∧ (¬x ∧ ¬y))

A lattice version of the characterisation is thereby not open but refuted. That is more than "we have not proved it": exactly the pattern that the characterisation excludes on linear scales is assemblable here. The limit is no hole in the proof but a counterexample — and the breaking point is exactly the lattice shape an architecture of equal-ranking roles would have.

That the architecture can state its own height of fall is no admission but its strongest piece. It stands on order, not on plurality, and it can say by what one notices the difference.

And the theorem that supports the posit of chapter 2 belongs in the same context: on an incomparable pair even the minimum leaves the two-element set [49]. The same property that grounds the posit is the one whose absence makes the bound fall.

The four limits

First, mathematical. The characterisation lives on linearly graded carriers and falls on lattices of equal-ranking elements — refuted, as just shown, not unclarified.

Second, hermeneutic. That the two-element value set is Günther's elementary contexture is decided by no theorem. The posit stands on grounded and, since recently, theorem-supported soil — and remains a choice.

Third, in principle. Not the will as a function, but solely the that of enactment is no structural feature. There is no poorer model in which it is missing as a position, because it is not a position. Here the procedure declares its reach, as a statement about itself.

Fourth, at the subject matter: a coupling is not a ground. This limit is new and the heaviest of the four. The three previous ones concern the mathematics, the interpretation and the procedure; this one concerns the matter itself.

The construction shows a coupling of exchange and order, and in one direction: what is measured is the exchange against the ordering step — as a biconditional, on the ground, generically, over arbitrary pairs of places and without any normal-form assumption, with the stage-determining place as the breaking point. Günther claims more, and he claims it verbatim: the proemial relationship, he writes, "'pre-faces' the symmetrical exchange relation and the ordered relation and forms … their common basis"; it "provides a deeper foundation of logic as an abstract potential from which the classic relations of symmetrical exchange and proportioned order emerge."

Between the two there is no difference of degree. Whoever shows a coupling of two things has not shown that they stem from a third — and that would hold even if the coupling were mutual; here it is not even that. No strengthening of the first statement leads to the second. What is missing here is therefore not to be replaced by more of what has been built — and that distinguishes this limit from the other three, at each of which a further theorem would be conceivable.

That Günther's own footnote cites, for the same subject, Karl Heim's name for it — das Grundverhältnis, literally the ground relation — is no play on words of this paper but the very circumstance at issue here.

What is not built

The proemial relation. It is the pivot of Günther's late theory — and not his invention: he calls it implicit in Hegel's dialectic and points to a book by the theologian Karl Heim, Das Weltbild der Zukunft (1904), where it is called das Grundverhältnis. The name comes from the Greek prooimion, prelude.

In him it governs the connection of cognition and volition: a connection that "is in its inner core heterarchical and governed by the proemial relation" (C&V p. 33) — precisely the figure whose impossibility in every ordering chapter 3 proves. What is denied in this is named by two writings, and they deny different things: Cognition and Volition denies that either of the two faculties firmly dominates the other (p. 33); Identität, Gegenidentität und Negativsprache denies a symmetrical exchange relation between them and sets number, not the proemial relation, as the medium for cognition and volition (IGN p. 53). And what it establishes, he writes, is "a heterarchical connection between Form and Matter and consequently between subject and object and therefore also between volition and cognition."

Whether I and Thou are co-ordinate in Günther he says differently at different times. In 1963 the I stands above the Thou, not by the place values but by a numeral: "Nevertheless, the reflexive superordination of the 'I' over the 'Thou' is expressed in Table (VII) too! This happens, however, not by means of the place values that the individual two-valued systems possess relative to one another, but through the reflection numeral that belongs to the 'I'-system." (our translation) (Das Bewusstsein der Maschinen, 2nd ed., p. 176, checked on the page image). In 1977 he writes: "The irreducibility of I and Thou is an insight one reaches only when one grasps that I and Thou represent an exchange relation within subjectivity. An exchange relation, however, is not an order relation in which the one member could ever be superordinated to the other." (our translation) (IuG p. XXVIII, checked on the page image; the italics are Günther's). How the two places relate to each other, this text leaves open. Kaehr and Ditterich read it in 1979 such that order remains within each system and exchange happens on change of system: "The four-place proemial relation represents the connection between subject and object in such a way that, on the one hand, the order relation between subject and object or operator and operand is maintained within each logical system, but that, on change of system, what was previously subject now becomes object, or what was I now becomes Thou, and vice versa." (our translation) (Kaehr and Ditterich 1979, edition pp. 15 f., checked on the page image). That is their reading, and the text carries it as a reading.

Günther writes down both orders of rank, for a single subjectivity: "we have to contend with two inverse hierarchical (ordered) relations. In one case the outside world is on the apex of the hierarchy and rules unconditionally over the subject and in the reverse hierarchical relation the subject is sovereign and reigns supreme over the object. It is obvious that in the first case subjectivity will appear to us as a cognitive system." (C&V p. 11, checked on the page image). That the symmetry which in cognition is subjectivity becomes in volition a property of the world, offering the will an alternative, we read from two further places, at which order arises from choice (p. 16) and the reversal of the order of subject and object stands (p. 23); that is our synopsis. Where this text speaks of the will and heterarchy, it carries Günther's own weakening along: "We know only in a very vague way that a very close affinity seems to exist between will and heterarchy … and an analogous one between thought and hierarchical structure." (our translation) (IuG p. XXX, read in the text layer; the ellipsis is Günther's).

What it makes reversible, Günther says himself: "Aristotle did claim that materiality – his hyle – is the sole potential which becomes actual and real only by assuming a form. But the proemial relation implies that we are equally entitled to say that Form is a mere potentiality which must be filled with content in order to become Reality." (C&V p. 22, checked on the page image). Which side is the merely possible, form or matter, is reversible in him; the word "reversible" is our compression. In place of Aristotle's fixed order of rank there steps a heterarchical connection.

The error on which this hangs Günther names in 1977: "The simple exchange relation of affirmativity and negation, with which classical logic begins, was in its application to the world always interpreted as a relation of rank or preference, in which the one component was unconditionally super- or subordinated to the other." (our translation) (IuG p. XXV, checked on the page image).

Two cautions belong to the proemial relation, and they are unequal.

The first concerns the language, and it falls away. Günther wrote Cognition and Volition in English; both editions of this paper quote the original.

The second concerns the bridge to chapter 3, and it is to be divided. Documented is that the cyclic order of preference belongs, in Günther, to heterarchy and heterarchy to the proemial relation — in the same essay he builds a cyclic order of preference over three values, calls it heterarchical, and remarks that two values do not have enough structure for such a distinction. What remains interpretation is only that the three corners of 1937 are the same as the late faculties: there thought, will and time, here two faculties and a relation of rank, and a good three decades between them.

Read this way the arc of this paper closes: chapter 3 shows the phenomenon, this chapter surveys Günther's answer to it — and says how far it carries formally.

The proemial relation is not built as a whole. What is built is more than a section and less than the matter.

The stage movement carries a section property and an asymmetry of fibres and does not collapse into an invertibility: the descent is canonical, the ascent a choice [90] — and that it is one stands as a theorem and not as a remark, because two admissible ascents demonstrably come apart [91].

The chiasm of exchange and order — Günther's interlocking, and the word is his: the proemial relation, he writes, "represents a peculiar interlocking of exchange and order" — the German translation renders it Ineinandergreifen, which is why the German edition must fix a word of its own and this one need not. What is meant is the coupling of the two fundamental relations. It is one-sidedly touched and not redeemed.

Touched, and the direction belongs to the information: what is measured is the exchange against the ordering step — the swapping of two places, measured against the striking of the last. If it does not touch the stage-determining place, the two operations commute smoothly; if it does, the ordering step stays the same exactly under a statable condition. That the breaking point is precisely the stage-determining place is the yield and no incidental. And the converse direction is missing: no theorem measures the order against the exchange.

Not redeemed: the simultaneous reversal of the order of rank. Günther says it himself and expressly: the switch involves "not two relata but four! Not only two subjective faculties, called cognition and volition, are exchanged, but the order of subject and object also suffers a reversal." What is missing is therefore precisely the representable exchange of cognition and volition itself.

A second openness, at the same place and different in kind. The missing converse direction concerns the coupling of the two fundamental relations. This one concerns their occupancy: which of the two relations a value occupancy of two kenograms carries.

The separation theorem. Since 9 August the construction carries, at this place, a classification theorem, and it says more than the open place requires: every pair of criteria that divides the different-valued occupancies of two places exclusively and exhaustively, and stays stable under order embeddings of the carrier, is the order pair — in one of its two namings and in no third form; proved [92]. Whoever wants to separate the two occupancies thereby has no choice but to rename the order relation.

The theorem and the three statements from which it draws its separating power manage without the axiom of choice. The normal form carries it; within the module no theorem carries the axiom of choice that does not name it — measured on the axiom profile of every theorem of the module, with the gate run green.

And from this follows what the chapter needs. The theorem leaves open which of the two occupancies carries the symmetrical exchange relation and which the order: its conclusion is a disjunction of two namings that it does not resolve, and both branches are of equal right. It therefore does not close the place — it proves that the place cannot be closed within this frame, and thereby confirms precisely the openness this paragraph started from.

Its reach it carries itself, and the reach belongs to the statement: two places, and as structure on the stock of values nothing beyond a linear order. More places and more structure stay expressly outside. The theorem does not say that there is no substantial value criterion — it says that within this frame there is none. Whether the bound is one of the matter or one of the frame, it does not decide.

What this has to do with Günther stands beside it and not in it. That the two places are his two kenograms with their value occupancy, and the two cases the symmetrical exchange relation and the order, are assignments from a single passage, and the words are his own: "the two empty squares represent kenograms which can either be filled in such a way that the value occupancy represents a symmetrical exchange relation or in a way that the relation assumes the character of an order" (assignment; Cognition and Volition, p. 22).

That this occupancy stays open in him, and not merely unmentioned, no passage says alone. At the figure itself he gives the disjunction and no rule; on p. 13, however, he sets up the rule by which the silence can be read as intention — he distinguishes "the mere structure of a symmetrical exchange relation and its two aspects of possible value-occupancy", and a value occupancy is not meant "unless we say so expressly" (synopsis; pp. 22 and 13 — neither passage says it alone; the evidence for p. 13 has been checked once. This note appears only where a piece of evidence has been checked once; its absence elsewhere means nothing.). Where he names none, by his own rule none is meant.

Not open, by contrast, is what he assigns himself elsewhere: "Equivalence, of course, is a symmetrical exchange and implication an ordered relation" (checked against the source; p. 32). What is open is the occupancy of the two kenograms, not the assignment of the two actualisations.

The role exchange itself is not built. In its place stands a differential: over every carrier whose stock of values carries a fixed-point-free exchange — hence over Günther's own table of negation — the relations cannot be named by their members [96]; and one order up a morphism becomes an object [97]. The exchange enforces the order; the order does not perform the exchange.

The generalisation holds for transpositions — the commutation theorem holds over arbitrary pairs of places and even over arbitrary rows, without any normal-form assumption [77]. For arbitrary permutations nothing stands.

And both strands of the construction instantiate the same form of proof — retraction without invertibility —; there is now a proof term that reaches both [98].

That Günther locates the proemial relation itself at the level of the kenogrammatic structures — as pure possibility that becomes actual only as the exchange or as the ordering relation, written with two kenograms — is the reason why the load-bearing theorems lie there and nowhere else (checked against the source).

The compound contexture is half built, and the halves can be named. The joining stands as structure: several elementary contextures exactly from three values on [46], their connectedness through overlap or a third [47], their coming apart exactly from four on [48]; and its exponent is a theorem [65]. The mediation does not stand: Günther binds it to the second negation, and no theorem of this paper carries that.

A limit object of the ascent is neither built nor claimed nor excluded. These three negations have three different reaches, and they are not the same.

And the two paragraphs likewise say different things. The first says what is missing and why it is missing; the second says that nothing has been said. A gap with a reason is worth more than a gap without — which is why halving the first negation is a yield and not a retreat. Up to the fifth edition the gap was called the compound contexture is not built; it is now called the joining is built, the mediation is not, and the second negation is the reason.

To the counting of compound contextures belongs a note that lies in the matter itself: the source gives a formula and a series beginning at one, and says at the same time that a compound contexture requires at least three values. The two do not agree — the first compound contexture is the three-valued one, the one belongs to the series of ontologies. The architecture counts the elementary contextures of an m-valued situation as pairs from m values; this is compatible with Günther's series, but it is a stipulation and not a derivation.

The picture

A ground that counts repetition, whose normal form is built as a procedure and whose value-filling has two proved bridges. Logics whose separation is not posited but proved — in a form that withstands Günther's own basic case, because it does not deny the map but the path to it from within. A mode of proof that knows its hard half and delivers it. A measure of separation that has turned from an existence claim into a characterisation, and from computed numbers into theorems. An ascent that is everywhere inhabited, takes itself along, grows strictly — and provably has no form that would close it. A layer of application that says where it bites. And four limits — three of them statements of place at which a further theorem would be conceivable, and one that says what this construction is not.

What is posited stands at the beginning; what is proved is counted; what is interpretation is called so.

This report is not another reading of Günther. It says what of Günther's and Kaehr's constructions has become operational, and where not. The word is Günther's; it stands in the title of his Beiträge zur Grundlegung einer operationsfähigen Dialektik (Contributions to the foundation of an operational dialectic). Here it means three things: proved, in a proof environment that checks every theorem; executable, as a check a reader can run; checkable, at the state this report names. Checkable means, on the day of this edition: building it oneself, with the commands and targets the README names. A check that runs publicly with every change does not exist yet. Every statement carries its provenance in words. It says whether Günther gives it, whether Kaehr, Mahler or Pfalzgraf give it, or whether we stipulate, read, assign or prove it.

Three names stand before every claim.

Günther ordered his work in phases himself. In the preface to the second edition of Idee und Grundriss einer nicht-Aristotelischen Logik he looks back in 1977 on the first volume: "When he concluded the first volume, his basic formula still read: two-valuedness is the classical; everything that appears beyond this frame already lies in the trans-classical. It is necessary today to admit that this judgement contains a considerable ontological misdirection of thought." (our translation) (IuG p. XXVII, checked on the page image). Where this report quotes Günther, it says from which year the sentence comes. And Günther said himself how far a formalisation of his subject can reach. The theory of dialectics, he writes in 1970, must be "progressively accessible to a process of formalisation", and further: "We say 'progressively', for the totality of the dialectical process, which reaches from absolute generality to the last isolated individuality, will remain unformalisable in toto. Mathematically speaking: the conditions of formalisation of dialectics must be recursive." (our translations) (HKN p. 14, read in the text layer). This report claims nothing beyond this limit.

Kaehr distinguished two forms of the proemial relation, an open and a closed one. Günther himself credits him with it, in 1977: "Mr Kaehr has since discovered that there are two proemial relations, which he calls 'open' and 'closed' respectively." (our translation) (IuG p. XXIX, checked on the page image), and so does Mahler in 1993. Mahler gave it, in his Morphogrammatik of 1993, a shape that can be built, under Kaehr's supervision. The limit of this shape Mahler named himself: in a classical system sameness is presupposed, not gained. The report tells both in O1. Whoever knows Kaehr should see on this page that we know him, and where this construction borders on him: at the calculus of 1981 (O3, O4), at the coupling of 1993 (O1), at the two directions of the circle (O7).

Pfalzgraf gave polycontextural logic a semantics in 1991, as an outsider, as he says himself: "It should be emphasized here that I am a non-specialist in PCL and not a member of the PCL group." (Pfalzgraf 1991, p. 170, checked on the page image by two hands). It consists of a free parallel system of three two-valued fibres and Günther's three-valued system as its quotient (Pfalzgraf 1991, pp. 172, 175, checked on the page image by two hands). O2 to O6 hang on this semantics.

Only then the claim. These constructions stand here proved; a prover runs; every statement carries its provenance. And two limits, here at once and kept apart, so that no reader expects what the report does not give.

The first limit is that of extension. In the foundation of this construction a higher-order function sees of its argument only the table; there is more only at representations. That is proved [106], and Part C says where Günther's text points here.

The second limit is that of sameness. The same member in two places, once operator, once operand, can be represented in a classical system only by carrying the places explicitly. Sameness is then presupposed, not gained. This holds for this construction as for Mahler's implementation, and Mahler says it of his own (O1). The two limits are different. A term lies beyond the table but on this side of sameness; the report keeps them apart and does not reach "the same limit from two sides".

From this follows the sentence that must stand early: the proemial relation itself is not in the corpus. What is there are shapes that Kaehr, Mahler and Pfalzgraf have given it or its surroundings, and the places where Günther himself sees more than the table. Whoever is disappointed by this should be so here and not only at the end.

What follows are seven sections. O1 the coupling; O2 Pfalzgraf's fibering; O3 Kaehr's separating case and validity; O4 Kaehr's calculus; O5 Günther's implications and transjunction; O6 the bridge between two layers of the corpus; O7 the small figure with which Günther speaks three times. The appendix in Part B resolves every number.

O1 · The coupling — Kaehr's distinction in Mahler's model

In his Morphogrammatik (Klagenfurt 1993) Thomas Mahler gives the proemial relation a shape, in a chapter he calls "Modellierung der Proemialrelation" (modelling the proemial relation). The title page of the book names him alone; the work arose in a research project under the direction of Rudolf Kaehr, who, according to Mahler's acknowledgement, supervised it, and the edition stands in the Kaehr archive under both names. A combinator couples two applications, (f x) and (g y). The distinction on which he builds he credits to Kaehr: "Kaehr distinguishes the open and closed form of the proemial relation." (our translation) (PDF p. 226 (edition p. 214), checked on the page image, with a reference to Kaehr's materials of 1973–75; the italics are Mahler's). His definition: "In general, for PR(f, g, x, y): if x ≡z g, then the two computations (f x) and (g y) are proemially coupled (in the open form). If in addition y ≡z f, the closed proemial relation is present. If, as in the example above, f ≡z g ≡z x ≡z y, the structure is autoproemially closed." (our translation) (PDF p. 231 (edition p. 219), checked on the page image). The sign ≡z stands for pointer equality: the same object at the same place in memory, not merely the same term. What is built here is this shape of a coupling, after Mahler, with Kaehr's distinction. It is not the proemial relation itself.

What the coupling depends on, Mahler says himself: "Which kind of proemial relation is present in the parallel evaluation of two combinator expressions (f x) and (g y) cannot be determined from the term structure but only from the structure of pointer equality (≡z) and pointer inequality (≢z) of f, x, g and y." (our translation) (PDF p. 233 (edition p. 221), checked on the page image). The difference that counts here he calls that of equality and sameness. Two terms can be equal without being selfsame, and of a calculus that knows only terms he writes: "A calculus operates only with types, and since type equality (or gestalt equality ≡sem) does not also imply token equality (i.e. physical or pointer equality ≡z), it cannot use any concept of sameness at all." (our translation) (PDF p. 227 (edition p. 215), checked on the page image). In this sense the report uses the word sameness.

His thesis stands proved as a theorem: whether two applications are coupled does not follow from their terms, only from the identifiers of their places, however deep one unfolds the places into terms [107]. In the corpus the applications are nodes of a graph, and every node carries an identifier; the identifier stands where pointer equality stands in Mahler; that is our assignment. The construction is calibrated against his count. He writes the equalities of the four places as kenograms of length 4 and finds five that designate a coupling; the others do not: "The remaining ten of the fifteen kenogram sequences of length 4, however, designate relations of pointer equality and inequality that do not satisfy the proemial relation (since x ≢z g, there is no exchange relation)." (our translation) (PDF p. 234 (edition p. 222), checked on the page image). Proved: of the fifteen kenograms of length 4, five are coupled, two closed, one self-coupled, and the kind of coupling stays the same under every renaming of the identifiers [108].

What separates the open form from the closed? In Cognition and Volition (lecture 1971, full text 1979) Günther orders the members of his relation by an index: "The subscript i signifies higher or lower logical orders." (C&V p. 21, read in the text layer). Kaehr says of the level on which the operand becomes operator that it is "neither super- nor subordinate to the preceding one, but co-ordinate" (our translation) (Kaehr 1990, PDF p. 11 (edition p. 10), checked on the page image). Proved: a closed coupling admits no grading, an open chain of any length admits one [109]. A grading here means a rank function under which every operator stands one level above its operand. That the theorem hits exactly the difference between Günther's index and Kaehr's co-ordination is our assignment.

And the limit Mahler names himself, this construction shares. The same member in two places, once operator, once operand, can be represented in a classical system only by carrying the places explicitly. Sameness is then presupposed, not gained. This holds for the identifiers of this construction as for the pointers of his implementation, and he says it of his own: "In contrast to the model above, which must presuppose the selfsame objects for the definition of the simultaneous exchange, in Günther's approach the concept of object (and in particular sameness) cannot be presupposed, since, in contrast to classical calculi, it dispenses with an a priori distinction subject:object." (our translation) (PDF p. 236 (edition p. 224), checked on the page image). The construction neither sharpens this limit nor overcomes it. Different from it is a second limit, that of extension; the entrance names both separately.

O2 · Pfalzgraf's fibering — the free system and its quotient

Pfalzgraf's semantics has two storeys. Below lies a free parallel system of three two-valued fibres, one for each of the three subsystems 1-2, 2-3 and 1-3. Above lies Günther's three-valued system, as the quotient of the free system. So Pfalzgraf describes it in 1991 (pp. 172, 175). Any two of the three subsystems have exactly one value in common, and everything else hangs on that.

In every fibre there are sixteen binary operations. A triple of such operations, one per fibre, is called admissible if it yields a well-defined operation in the quotient. Proved: a triple is admissible if and only if each of its three operations is idempotent, that is, returns the argument when applied to two equal arguments. In each fibre these are four of sixteen, in all 4 · 4 · 4 = 64 of 4096 triples [110]. The reason is that admissibility checks only the diagonal. Only there do two fibres read the same pair, and these two share exactly one value. Pfalzgraf saw the reason: in 1991 as a compatibility condition (p. 179), in 1995 and 2004 as a remark, without theorem and proof (1995 PDF p. 9; 2004 p. 222). The characterisation as idempotence per fibre, the number and the proof we have contributed, as far as the writings of Pfalzgraf we have read go. For his decomposition procedure Pfalzgraf 2004 refers to a work we have not read: "For a more detailed version with a short proof we refer to [16]." (p. 223, read in the text layer; No. 16 of his bibliography is the diploma thesis of W. Meixl, Salzburg 1999). What is proved there we do not know. Our theorem is calibrated against Pfalzgraf's own examples (1991, pp. 173, 180): conjunction, disjunction, conjunction is admissible; conjunction, disjunction, implication is not [111].

Here the proof meets Günther's own hand. His eight conjunctions and disjunctions from Cognition and Volition are admissible, and their quotient tables are his figures 7 to 14, cell by cell (C&V pp. 25 f., checked on the page image by two hands) [112]. The corpus carries the eight tables in three versions. That they are the same is proved [113].

The negators fit as well. The negators Pfalzgraf defines in the free system yield Günther's negators on three values in the quotient; that is proved [114].

Whether a formula is valid in the quotient is decided by an executable check, and it is proved that it decides exactly that [115]. A second check reports whether every connective of the formula is admissible, and it too is proved [116]. For an inadmissible formula the value in the quotient is a convention of the corpus. It says nothing about Pfalzgraf's quotient, and the theorems this report cites speak only of admissible formulas. The reader can see this for himself. In a reading file (Part B) he writes down a formula and gets the answer of the free system and that of the quotient side by side. If a connective is inadmissible, the file says so. Its examples are checked at every build.

O3 · Kaehr's separating case and validity

A formula is called valid here if it is true in all three subsystems at once; the values 1 and 2 count as true. We stipulate both. The second stipulation, the designation, stands against a sentence of Günther's. In Idee und Grundriss einer nicht-Aristotelischen Logik (1959) he describes how the logic of his time clings to the old: "This desperate clinging to the tradition of classical formalism shows itself even where one has lately begun to investigate many-valued systems." One of the forms he names: "one places the values in two groups of 'designated' and 'non-designated' values and stipulates that the designated values are to indicate truth". And at the beginning of the next paragraph: "It has not yet been grasped that the sense of meaning of a logic is determined by the sense of meaning of its values" (our translations) (IuG p. 105, checked on the page image; the italics are Günther's). The designation by which this report computes is therefore our choice and not Günther's. We say so here, where it is used.

Validity has a model. In shape it is Kaehr's universal validity of 1981: a formula is universally valid if each of its components lies in all truth sets of its subsystem (PDF p. 15 (edition p. 14), checked on the page image). For formulas with negators Kaehr fixes in which subsystem evaluation takes place not in this definition but in his rules; by them the value moves along (PDF p. 16 (edition p. 15), checked on the page image by two hands). That our validity and his coincide there too is, in this reading, an assignment. For Kaehr's calculus, corrected by one line of his rule table, the coincidence is proved [117]. That this line is a misprint is our reading.

In 1981 Kaehr named what he takes to be a misunderstanding in Günther. Through the choice of the truth-table method, he writes, in Günther "a self-misunderstanding has crept into the mathematising text and hardened: the identification of 'logical value' and 'truth value'". His example is a single formula. In Günther's formalisation of place-value logic it is universally valid, "this, however, only on the basis of an identification of the truth value of the statement and the place-value index of the truth values concerned"; by his tableau method it is not (our translations) (PDF p. 3 (edition p. 2), checked on the page image). The formula is N₅(p ∧∧∧ N₃p ∧∧∧ N₄p), a negator over a conjunction. N₃ and N₄ are concatenations of the two basic negators (PDF p. 17 (edition p. 16)); N₅ Kaehr does not determine at this place. The corpus sets N₅ = N₁N₂N₁, as Pfalzgraf gives it in 1991 "using the notational convention of PCL" (p. 177, checked on the page image). That Kaehr's N₅ is this one is our assignment. In Pfalzgraf's picture we read Kaehr's distinction as that between the free system, in which every value belongs to its fibre, and the quotient, in which values of different fibres are glued into one. That is our reading, checked at his formula and not proved in general.

Proved: in the quotient, Günther's three-valued system, the formula is valid; its value is constantly 1. In the free system it is not. At this formula the valuations that make it false are exactly the incoherent ones [118]. That "incoherent" matches Pfalzgraf's residue classes is our assignment. Kaehr's finding of 1981 thus stands as a theorem, calibrated against his own formula.

O4 · Kaehr's calculus

In 1981 Kaehr gave a tableau calculus, with rules for conjunction, disjunction, implication and the two negators (rule table PDF p. 16 (edition p. 15)). Proved: for the free system this calculus is sound and complete, and the prover delivers for every formula a closure or a counter-valuation [119]. Kaehr's rules stand in it verbatim, with one exception. One line of the rule table, printed "t³XN₁X → f²X", is corrected. That it is a misprint is our reading; it rests on the pattern of the N₂ lines of the same table (checked on the page image by two hands). Three things we have added, and they do not belong to Kaehr: a rule that treats the remaining thirteen operations from their tables; provability in the version his own example requires; and arbitrarily many variables.

Two of Kaehr's examples stand as theorems [120]. His negation cycle is provable. At the separating case the prover delivers the two open branches Kaehr draws as counter-valuations (PDF pp. 16 f. (edition pp. 15 f.)). What is compared is his result, not his drawing.

Kaehr's printed definition of provability is not sound: it calls a formula provable that is not valid in the free system. That is proved, against its wording [121] (PDF p. 16 (edition p. 15)). By our calculation it never checks one subsystem. The page itself does not say so.

What Kaehr himself writes about the state of his calculi we have read in two writings. In the report by Bashford, Joemann and Kaehr, Tableaux-Beweiser für polykontexturale Logik mit Transjunktionen (Dortmund 1993), it says: "The rule set described here is a first approach and is not yet complete and correct." (our translation) (PDF p. 5, checked on the page image). Of the term rules for transjunctional formulas Kaehr writes in 2005: "They are still waiting for a strict mathematical proof of correctnes!" (PDF p. 67, checked on the page image; "correctnes" thus in the print). Proved here is Kaehr's calculus for the junctional part, in the free system [119]. The transjunctional rules Kaehr calls open this construction does not prove.

At this point the construction is first of all a catching-up. Generic, complete tableau and sequent systems for finite-valued logics have existed on paper since Rousseau and Takahashi 1967, Carnielli 1987 and Hähnle 1990. The system MUltlog generates calculi with proofs on paper, and Marcos in 2010 deposits generated rules as axioms in Isabelle (according to our search; C.7 names its reach). For Kaehr's calculus this section catches up with the state of these works. What it brings in addition is the form: the proof is machine-checked.

O5 · Günther's implications and transjunction

In 1970 Günther introduces transjunction and distinguishes its grades. He begins with the place where it breaks in, the place "which is contexturally weakest because the variables differed in their value occupation" (our translation) (HKN PDF p. 20, edition A/1 — 4, checked on the page image by two hands). In each of the three subsystems there are two such places, six in all. Then the grades: "The transjunction given above is total. It occurs without exception at all places at which there is the possibility of rejecting a value alternative distributed over p and q." And further: "The whole three-valued system, however, naturally also contains functions in which such a rejection occurs only for one or for two contextures. Finally, it is possible that the rejection is intra-contexturally partial; i.e., it may occur when, e.g., p has the negative value, but not when the negative value is carried by q." (our translations) (the same page, checked on the page image by two hands). The second grade we call contextural; the word is our naming. The predicates with which the corpus captures the three grades are ours, after Günther's wording. Proved: his total transjunction breaks in at all six weak places, his eight conjunctions and disjunctions at none [122].

Günther's implications from Cognition and Volition do not belong to the junctional quotient. This follows from three theorems of the corpus; it does not stand as a theorem of its own [123]. By Günther's own concept of 1970 they are intra-contexturally partial transjunctions. The break-in stands in subsystem 2-3 at the cell (3, 2), where p carries the negative value and q the positive, and never at (2, 3) [124]. It comes from Günther's rule for implication, not from the functors (C&V pp. 29 f.). Implications from transjunctive functors C&V only defers; there is no contradiction with 1970. His tables from Cognition and Volition and his concept of 1970 are thereby connected by a theorem.

For one of these tables there are predecessors. One of Günther's eight standard implications, C&V fig. 20, is, after each one's own renaming of the values, the same table as the IMPLY in the introductory example of two of Pfalzgraf's writings. Both follow Rydeheard and Burstall 1988, p. 169, in this: Pfalzgraf and Sofronie 1995 (PDF p. 4, checked on the page image) and Pfalzgraf 2004 (p. 221, checked on the page image). That the tables are the same is our assignment, after their renaming. Both show that the table needs a transjunction in the second subsystem if it is to be compatible with the identification of values that AND and OR yield; with its own identification it decomposes without transjunction (1995 PDF p. 8, checked on the page image; 2004 pp. 221 f., read in the text layer). The true outputs of this subsystem then go to T1 (1995 PDF p. 9, checked on the page image; 2004 p. 222, checked on the page image). The identification that AND and OR yield in Pfalzgraf and Sofronie is the same on which our quotient stands: T1 and T3, F1 and T2, F2 and F3 each become one value (1995 PDF p. 7, checked on the page image; for the corpus Pfalzgraf 1991, pp. 172, 175). They read it off AND and OR; the corpus sets it after Pfalzgraf 1991 and then shows that Günther's eight conjunctions and disjunctions are compatible with it (O2). With Günther's tables they connect the example in neither of the two writings; this is measured over both writings in full, with a search that recognises umlauts. Pfalzgraf and Sofronie do not name Günther at all in 1995. Pfalzgraf names him in 2004 as the originator of polycontextural logic (pp. 213 and 215) and lists two of his writings in the bibliography (p. 226), not at the example (pp. 218–222). The name of transjunction Pfalzgraf takes expressly from polycontextural logic (p. 217). For its definition both writings refer, at the example, to Pfalzgraf's own earlier work.

Beyond these predecessors the construction goes at three places. First, it shows this for all eight standard implications (C&V figs. 20–27) [125]. Second, it locates the break-in at the one cell (3, 2). In Pfalzgraf, counted at the table, three cells leave the subsystem, in both writings. Proved: the difference from Günther's one cell is exactly the two diagonal cells whose value another subsystem reads as its true value [126]. Third, it shows that the eight are of the third grade of transjunction that Günther determined in 1970, exactly after his example; this is proved at predicates after Günther's wording [127]. The connection of the implications from C&V with the grades from HKN is our synopsis. That Günther himself classifies his implications so, he does not say in the six writings we have read with this in view.

The transjunction of the older layer of the corpus is likewise of the third grade, mirrored, in subsystem 1-3; that is proved [128]. It is a second case of the same grade, at another place.

O6 · The bridge

Two layers of the corpus arose independently of each other. The oldest, the clone layer, calls a table on three values contexture-faithful if it preserves all three elementary contextures. The youngest is Pfalzgraf's fibering. Proved: a table on three values preserves all three elementary contextures if and only if it is the quotient table of an admissible triple after Pfalzgraf. These are 64 of 3⁹ = 19 683 tables [129]. The reason is the same as in O2, seen from the other side. Every diagonal cell is read by two subsystems that share exactly one value, every weak place by exactly one [130].

Decomposition and composition Pfalzgraf describes: 1991 (pp. 180 f.), 2004 as a procedure with a remark (pp. 219–223), 1995 the condition only as a remark, without theorem and proof. The equality with the concept of the older layer, the number and the proof are ours; the proof does without an enumeration of the tables. What Pfalzgraf describes as condition and procedure stands proved here. For the procedure he refers to a short proof in Meixl, which we have not read (O2).

O7 · The small figure — one circle, two senses of rotation, two paths

This section is the only one in which the report becomes philosophical. It deals with a small figure that runs through three of Günther's texts. It is a circle on three values that can be traversed in two senses of rotation, and there are two paths that lead to the same result. It is one circle, not two: two sequences of negations traverse it in opposite directions. That the three texts speak of the same figure is suggested by the same words and equations; Günther says it expressly nowhere. That is our synopsis.

1970. In Die historische Kategorie des Neuen Günther gives the negation table for three values. "For columns 4 and 5 two negation operations are necessary, and the last column needs three, but can, as one sees, be produced in two ways." (our translation) The values that do not change he replaces by a stroke (HKN PDF p. 24, edition A/2 — 4, checked on the page image). The two paths are N₁.₂.₁ and N₂.₁.₂. He writes the table down a second time and now sets the stroke at the value the last step holds fixed: "Depending on which negation operation one prefers, a different value remains constant." (our translation) And he says why the two paths matter to him: "But since Hegelian logic includes the problem of time (and mediation is essentially a problem of the combination of time and timeless logic), the genesis of this result must be important to us." (our translation) (PDF p. 25, edition A/2 — 5, checked on the page image; Genese is letter-spaced in the print). Genesis is here bound to time and mediation. In the corpus the stroke is calibrated: on one path the fixed place stands above, on the other below, as in Günther [131].

1979. In Identität, Gegenidentität und Negativsprache (Belgrade 1979) Günther determines p by two sequences of six negations, N₁·₂·₁·₂·₁·₂ and N₂·₁·₂·₁·₂·₁, and writes: "To these equivalences correspond value permutations that form Hamilton circles." (our translation) (IGN p. 18, checked on the page image). On the next page: "It is readily apparent that the two interpretations of p in the space of negativity differ in that we have run through the circle once clockwise and once in the opposite direction." (our translation) (p. 19, checked on the page image). Then he decomposes the circle into the two paths. In the first permutation group the value sequence 1·2·3 becomes the sequence 3·2·1 through three negations, in the second through N₂·₁·₂; the way back goes in the first through N₂·₁·₂, in the second through N₁·₂·₁ (p. 19). For the outward path of the first group the page prints "N 1⋅2⋅3". With three values there is no third negator, and the page itself requires N₁·₂·₁ (Part A). And he names what distinguishes the two paths: "Time plays a role here insofar as the negation sequences N 1⋅2⋅1⋅2⋅1⋅2 and N 2⋅1⋅2⋅1⋅2⋅1 are always a path of reflection which runs forward in time and which, through the different order of the negations, can take on two shapes, which in turn produce an ambiguity. And insofar as the end product of the reflections that have taken place is a result of the path the reflection has travelled, it can, qua end product, be understood and distinguished only through its course of becoming." (our translation) (p. 20, checked on the page image).

The narrowest circle. On p. 41 Günther says why there are six steps: "It is obvious that the 6 steps of negativity in the three-valued, N₁·₂·₁·₂·₁·₂ or N₂·₁·₂·₁·₂·₁, cannot be reduced if the negation sequence is not to lose its cyclic character. The circle of three-valuedness is therefore the narrowest, and it has 6 stations." (our translation) (IGN p. 41, checked on the page image by two hands). In the corpus a circle is a sequence of negations that returns to its start, visits no station twice and is more than the mere there-and-back of a single negator. Proved: on three values every such circle has six steps, it runs through all arrangements, and it is one of Günther's two sequences [134]. That Günther's "cyclic character" means this circle is our assignment. That he does not call the mere there-and-back a circle is our reading of his sentence; he does not say it. That on three values no circle is shorter than six, on the other hand, he says himself, at another place: in the triadic, the difference between a circle "at which not all" and another "at which all permutations of the negative take part" does "not yet" exist (our translation) (p. 57, checked on the page image by two hands; the italics are Günther's).

Table IX. Immediately afterwards the doctrine seems to fall. "But if one passes to four-valuedness, a wealth of new problems opens up that could not at all be anticipated in triadic structures. Thus, e.g., four-valuedness seems to reject the doctrine of three-valuedness that the smallest heterarchical circle must have 6 negation stations." (our translation) As counter-examples Günther adduces two sequences of four steps, N₁·₃·₁·₃ and N₃·₁·₃·₁ (Table IX). Below the table he takes them back: "But only a very superficial glance at the table can be deceived. Since the connecting second negation is missing, this is not at all a genuine four-valued operation, but two relatively independent exchange relations, which give the impression of a cyclic structure only because the procedures are carried out in such an order that, when an exchange has been performed with one pair of values, it may not be taken back before the other pair of values has undergone the same action. This has nothing specifically to do with four-valuedness. One could, e.g., replace the value number 3 in Table IX by a million and the value number 4 by its immediate successor. Nothing would be changed by that. Even then we would obtain a pseudo-circle with four stations of negating." (our translation) (IGN p. 41; the sentence above the table checked on the page image by two hands, the table and the text below it checked on the page image). The two sequences are pseudo-circles; the word is Günther's. The doctrine of the six stations he lets stand. Proved: on four values there are, without N₂, exactly two circles in the sense of the corpus, and they are Günther's two pseudo-circles. Every circle in which N₂ occurs has at least six steps [135]. The circles with six steps are the three-valued ones that leave one of the four values fixed; which ones they are is proved [136], that they exist is calculated. Günther's doctrine thus holds there in a reading of his criterion: "the connecting second negation" means that N₂ occurs in the sequence. The reading is ours, and it is framed only for four values. It has a support in Günther himself, in one direction. Seven pages later he rejects the same sequence "because through the absence of the mediating N 2 only a semblance of a four-valued systematics arises" (our translation) (p. 48, checked on the page image). No four-valued circle without N₂: that he says himself, at this sequence. The converse he does not say. Two sentences further on he sets the minimum higher: "If we now ask ourselves what the minimal extent of a four-valued circle can be, the cycle with eight stations of negativity results as a matter of course." (our translation) (p. 48, checked on the page image). Our "at least six" is therefore correct, but it is not Günther's minimum. Proved: if all three negations occur, every circle on four values has at least eight steps, as Günther says, and his Table XVIII has exactly eight [137]. The premise "all three" is our reading of his "four-valued circle". The theorem is calibrated against Günther's Table XVIII and at his sequence (26) (IGN p. 48, checked on the page image) [138]. The circle concept of the corpus is, from four values on, wider than Günther's heterarchical circle, for it catches the pseudo-circles. That is why the reading stands in the theorem and not in the concept.

1980. In Martin Heidegger und die Weltgeschichte des Nichts Günther writes down the two equations again. "The two negation sequences are completely equal in rank, and there is no theoretical reason to prefer the one to the other. In both equations we are dealing with a negation relation that runs back into itself, that is, with a circle which, like all circles, can be traversed in a double sense of rotation, and there is no theoretical authority in the whole universe that could tell us which sense of rotation should be preferred to the other. On the other hand, however, the identity of p can only be established when it has been decided that p is to be interpreted in the sense of the first or of the second equation. All identity accessible to theoretical and subsequent thinking is thus grounded in an entirely untheoretical act that precedes it." (our translation; the print reads theoretische und nachträglichem, which we take as 'theoretical and subsequent thinking'; read as printed, it says 'all identity that is theoretical and accessible to subsequent thinking') (HWN pp. 26 f., checked on the page image). The word Drehsinn, sense of rotation, is Günther's. The pages count by the edition of 2005; the text first appeared in 1980 in Nachdenken über Heidegger (ed. U. Guzzoni, Hildesheim), reprinted in the same year in the Beiträge, vol. 3, pp. 260–296. A related thought Günther had already written in 1979, on the same page as the sentence about the course of becoming, a few sentences after it: "Presumably at this point the reader who has followed the line of thought presented so far will want to consider which of the two negation sequences written down leads to the conception of object identity and which to that of subject identity. It must be pointed out, however, that a trinitarian logic gives us no hint how this is to be answered. Since the two negation sequences under discussion stand in a relation of symmetry, identity and counter-identity form an exchange relation in which each can take the place of the other without anything changing ontically." (our translation) (IGN p. 20, checked on the page image). That 1979 and 1980 mean the same thing is our synopsis. The reach differs: in 1979 "a trinitarian logic" gives no hint, in 1980 there is no authority "in the whole universe". What Günther says next in the same paragraph about I and Thou this report does not take up here; C.8 quotes I and Thou only as Günther's example of "constitutive".

The connection of the two directions of revolution with time, course of becoming and an untheoretical decision thus stands in Günther himself, from 1970 to 1980. Kaehr takes it up in 1982 and widens it. In his words it is the "sense of direction of the cycles", their "running to the right or to the left", and in the triadic system "two paths" are possible. Then he goes beyond the three values, to all paths of the many-valued negation systems: "their often manifoldly entangled paths through negativity, through meontics, imply a history. Which path was chosen is motivated by experiences that do not stem from the negation system; here cognitive and volitive processes cross, that is, meontics and kenogrammatics." (our translation) (Kaehr 1982, PDF pp. 41 f. (edition pp. 40 f.), checked on the page image). That this binding includes the two triadic directions, as its first case, is a narrow reading. Kaehr names the two triadic directions in the paragraph before.

What the corpus contributes. Which sense of rotation holds, the corpus does not say. It says that no criterion can choose it that respects the mirroring of the values, and what follows from each. Proved: the sequence in the opposite sense is the mirrored one of the other and runs through the same stations backwards, and no mirror-faithful feature separates the two [139]. Günther's "relation of symmetry" of 1979 we assign to the first part of this theorem, his missing "hint" to the second. The theorem of the corpus holds for three values; it thus stands at the reach of 1979, not at that of 1980. Beside Günther's sentence that there is no theoretical authority deciding the sense of rotation, there now stands this theorem. The bridge between the two is ours. Günther speaks of theoretical reasons, the theorem of features that respect the mirroring. Both speak of the two senses of rotation of the whole circle, not of the two paths.

For the two paths something else holds, and it is a calculation. With three values the common result of the two geneses is the mirroring of the values, that is, the exchange of Günther's 1 and 3. The mirroring carries the two negators into each other and with them the one genesis into the other. Proved is the carrying-over of the negators for every value number [140]. That the mirroring is the exchange of 1 and 3 holds only for three values. In the hexagon the same exchange is a half turn: every station goes over into the one that lies three steps further on in the same sequence. That is proved for both sequences, calculated separately for each [133]. The figure shows it.

The end belongs to Günther. One page after the judgement on the full cycles that Part C takes up, he asks a question that holds for every full cycle, also for the hexagon of this section. The corpus builds no spiral. The question is Günther's, and here it remains his: "If in the analysis of the Hamilton circles one is content that every full cycle returns self-sufficiently into itself, then in their construction one has completely ignored the problem of time. Put popularly: how do the cycles with growing or shrinking circumferences form themselves into a spiral?" (our translation) (IGN p. 58, checked on the page image by two hands).

2 · The mode of proof, in full

Part A presents the differential in its short form. Here stands what is obligatory about it.

2.1 The three marks

First: the poor model is built internally. A statement about "all orderings" or "everything buildable from the local means" must have its object inside the system. Claims in the metalanguage do not count, because they cannot be checked.

Second: the result is conditional. It stands relative to the posited premises — above all to the one from Part A, chapter 2. It does not compel the sceptic, it symmetrises him: from this can be reduced comes I posit the one encompassing logic, and that is itself a posit without precedence.

Third: what is proved is the form, never the naming. The file that carries the witness of non-uniformity is named after the formal property and not after the philosophical reading. This is no modesty but a precaution: a name that claims more than its theorem will be taken for the theorem at the next reading.

2.2 The provenance of the poor classes

Here, and not with the theorems, sits the burden of proof of the construction.

Canonical is the procedure: the concept of the buildable and its generation are standard clone theory and not invented for this purpose.

Chosen is the basis: these operations, this embedding. The objection that this choice is cut to fit is admissible. It is not answered but declared — and Part A, chapter 2 says why the choice has a substantive reason and where that reason is itself a theorem.

And once the poor class was replaced. For the differential of the role exchange the announced class was "every carrier whose roles are distinguished by type". It is not internalisable — it quantifies over type judgements of the ambient language, and an ill-formed term has no negatable proposition — and it would have been false: the corpus contains a counter-witness over a one-element stock of values. The class that took its place — every carrier whose stock of values carries a fixed-point-free exchange — is admissible not because it is convenient but because it is Günther's symmetric exchange relation. The replacement is named in the file header together with both reasons for the fall of the literal version.

2.3 The differential, applied to the workshop

In the working phase before the fourth edition the form was applied twice to the architecture itself.

To a theorem: the parity obstruction [73] has exactly the differential shape — the impossibility half over all maps without side conditions, the possibility half with an explicit witness [74], [75] beside it.

To a measurement: where an axiom profile is to be explained, the workshop has since required two versions differing in exactly one quantity. The four cases stand in §4.3.

3 · The ledger and the register

3.1 What the ledger achieves

Günther's concepts stand in a definitions edition, divided into paragraphs. Which concept has which formal carrier is machine-checked and stands in a file of its own — not in this paper. What stands here is the mechanism.

How many rows the ledger carries, how many of them have a carrier and how many paragraphs are covered stands in docs/kennzahlen.md.

How many carriers appear in more than one row, and how many rows are surplus as a result, is stated in the ledger's self-report, with the arithmetic; no tool checks that arithmetic. A sentence about it stood wrong in the self-report until the fourth edition, for five revisions — "eight carriers more than once" instead of "five carriers, eight surplus rows" — and none of the rules could see it, because they read rows and not sentences about rows.

3.2 The rules

Two bite at build time, the others at lint time:

  • R1, R2 (build): every named carrier resolves in the environment, and its carrier status matches the kind of its declaration.
  • R3 no assignment status theorem — an assignment status is editorial and never compiler-checked, and must not call itself so.
  • R4 carrier status open forces an empty carrier column.
  • R5 every paragraph of the definitions edition represented, except the source paragraph on the proemial relation.
  • R6 carrier status theorem forces a guard entry.
  • R7 every carrier row has exactly one matching reference in the reference file, and conversely.
  • R8 every row number occurs exactly once in both files.
  • R9 every theorem name in the limit column resolves and has exactly one entry in the reference file.

R3 through R9 set the exit code. The open rows stay open — among them the row on the world-picture concept (L06-1), which now refers to the ascent probes and still has no carrier. An open row is not a defect of the ledger but its achievement.

3.3 The register of posits

Posits do not disappear, they are registered. The load-bearing rule is not "the aggregate contains no posit" but no theorem of the aggregate hangs on a posit. A field of type true can enter no proof; why it must nevertheless be marked is an epistemic and not a logical argument: what stands in the aggregate reads as certified holdings.

Two classes are carried — placeholders with an exit criterion, constitutive posits with a justification — and one standing probe: no non-projection of the aggregate has the conclusion true, value zero, with a known counter-count on the projection side.

How many posit fields the corpus carries, and how they divide between the two classes, stands in the posit register of the repository.

Exactly two of them are constitutive. One of these was not found when the register of marks was first built, because its file header states its status in the language of the construction pattern and not in the language of the marks. The lesson stands in §5.2.

4 · The safeguards

4.1 The standing

The values stand in docs/kennzahlen.md, generated from the corpus, each with its route: constants checked by the gate, guards written and enforced, theorems, build jobs, declared gaps. What stands here is what they mean.

Equations between the values run along at generation, and if one falls, none of the numbers is to be used. The load-bearing one is: guards written = guards enforced + those lying outside the default targets. Two independent routes arrive at the same decomposition — one over the file tree, one over the import closures. And it is more than a check of itself: because the one route sees the working tree and the other the tracked files, it falls as soon as a module lies in the tree and is not tracked. A rule that formerly demanded care is now a condition that breaks.

The gate reports that the aggregate draws no sorryAx and that the whitelist is empty.

The figure "guards" counts guards, not guarded theorems. A guard applies to a theorem or to a module; a module with a guard removes all its theorems from the count of guard-free modules. No number of guarded theorems therefore follows from the number of guards. The numbers stand in the generated table, at the anchor of this edition; the theorems of the frozen path stand there as a number of their own beside the others and are not guarded. A guard for every statement outside the frozen path is decided and comes after this edition.

4.2 The four mechanisms

First, the axiom guards. For every load-bearing theorem the profile is measured and frozen as an expectation; if the proof basis shifts silently, the build breaks. The guard secures the basis, not the wording.

Second, the statement pins. A weakened theorem with the same profile would pass a guard. Pins nail down the full wording and break the build on definitional drift.

Third, the global gate (§4.1).

Fourth, the separation of ranges. The gate's assurance holds for the aggregate and for no other range. That a module runs along with every build does not mean it is gated: the probes on the proemial relation are built along, guarded and ungated — a situation for which the third edition had no language, and which the fourth described for the first time.

4.3 Measured axiom provenance

Measuring a profile is cheap; saying where an axiom comes from is a second measurement. It has a form: two versions differing in exactly one quantity. Four cases:

  • A monotonicity auxiliary of the library draws Classical.choice into every consumer; avoiding it via the value route left four theorems choice-free.
  • The closure of a decision procedure is inherited by the users of a definition — measured at the differential against a hand-written version with an identical value and a different bound justification: same values, different profiles.
  • The equality conversion on finite carriers draws Classical.choice through an instance chain; the case-distinction version of the same correspondence is free — measured on the same file, with only the conversion exchanged. Without this measurement a choice profile on the correspondence would have looked plausible and would have been located wrongly.
  • In the counting layer the axiom sits in the finiteness machinery itself; hence the dividing line in §1.7.

A fifth case belongs beside them, because it went the other way. In a later move a saved declaration would have inherited Classical.choice into the whole chain below it — the saving was withdrawn after measurement, and the chain is choice-free. A saving that changes the profile is no saving: it trades one declaration for an axiom in everything downstream, and it does so silently, because the saved line is visible and the inherited axiom only shows in a profile.

What is measured throughout are profiles and dividing lines. What is not measured is the path of the axiom into the term — the file headers say both, in that order.

4.4 Theorems in guard-free modules of the aggregate

A statement about guard density has a quantifiable reservation, and it stood nowhere until the fourth edition.

While ordering the corpus by subject matter, at the state 427b4b0, 115 theorems in 32 aggregate modules were found whose module carries no guard — the unit of the count is the module and not the theorem —, spread over three strands; outside these three every aggregate module with theorems carries at least one. Striking: they are exactly the three strands whose subjects occurred in no edition of the paper. This coincidence is measured, its cause is not — and without a route no explanation is offered here.

For the largest of the three — the layer on the interchange of form and content — the question has meanwhile been decided. Of the 43 theorems it then had (counted on 4 August, 3b2da94) only two lay in the closure of a guarded theorem; the exception for auxiliary lemmas therefore did not carry. In two stages (4feaf25, 4fdf931) 17 guards have been set, selected by the marks the corpus itself sets: four after the core named in the file header, eight after the module index, five after the concept the respective file carries in its title — these five expressly marked as discretionary, with the note that a later move on a better basis may reclassify.

Afterwards, at the state 4fdf931, 21 of 43 lie in a closure, all nine modules carry at least one guard, and all 17 measured profiles are identical — the strand is profile-uniform. A third stage will not be built: the marks of the corpus are exhausted, and to switch a criterion because it has run out would be exactly the error the selection was built against. The remaining 22 theorems are recorded by name and with their source situation in an accepted report; they are unguarded and are called so.

The running state stands in docs/kennzahlen.md and has moved twice since the survey above. It is measured, not computed — the unit is the module: whoever guards a module takes all its theorems out of the count, and therefore a number of guards can never yield a number of theorems.

4.5 What a foreign glance finds

Whoever searches the tree for the gap mark finds three numbers, and each answers a different question: raw word occurrences across the whole tracked corpus, occurrences in the source files, and affected declarations — the last of these all outside the aggregate. The first two stand in docs/kennzahlen.md; the third needs an environment query and is measured separately when needed. The third is the load-bearing one; the first two count prose along — freeze notes, doc strings, the phrase "free of it".

This quantity counts itself, and the rule for it stands in §5.1.

The frozen branch stays frozen, with the same thawing conditions; green means compiles there, not gap-free. It carries no guard; how many theorems it holds is counted in docs/kennzahlen.md at the anchor.

4.6 What the shape rests on

Ordering the corpus by subject matter produced a piece of information that no range table can give: the shape does not rest on the whole corpus. Three of the seven layers of Part A are carried solely by the directory on the proemial relation, two solely by the kenogrammatic layer; probes, calibration, frozen preparatory work and the assignment layers do not carry it.

This is no criticism of the rest — they are legitimate and are marked as such in their headers. It is the statement of range belonging to the sentence "this architecture claims…", and it has been missing for three editions.

5 · The instruments

Alongside the theorems something has arisen that knows nothing of Günther and could travel further: a reviewing practice for the formalisation of philosophical texts. Whoever formalises a thinker has a problem that this project has at every formalisation: the interpretation outruns the term. A theorem is built, its name is chosen, and the name claims more than the theorem. Against this gradient no resolutions help, only mechanisms.

That a prover runs, the reader can see for himself. In the corpus lies a reading file with examples. Whoever changes a line in it to a formula of his own and builds the target Beispiele gets both answers side by side: that of the free system, with a counter-valuation if there is one, per variable and fibre as T or F, and that of the quotient, with values in Günther's numbering. The notation of the formulas is ours; it follows Kaehr's signs for the connectives and Pfalzgraf's names for the negators. If a connective of the formula is inadmissible, the file says so and gives no value. The examples are checked at every build of the corpus; Kaehr's separating case and his negation cycle are among them. A line the reader changes is not checked. The README names the command.

5.1 The rules

The rule file of the corpus carries marked rules, each from a measured misstep; how many there are stands in docs/kennzahlen.md, counted there and not carried forward here. The most load-bearing:

A route carries a must and a must-not case, and its name boundary is a character class, not an assumption about the notation of the build. Violated three times in a row, each time differently; the sharpening of the second error was itself the third.

The stipulations of a specification are checked against each other, not only each on its own. Two individually correct stipulations can jointly force something neither demands — three times, at rising cost: first a tool exception, then a non-construction decision, then an axiom in a profile.

A statement of holdings is a pointer, not a list. Enumerating statements without a route go stale silently. In a single file nineteen identically built ones stood; they have been replaced by section pointers. The same healing at a heading, at a preamble and at a rule number.

Claims about the reachability of a location are write probes, not inferences from empty search results. A missing result looks like an answer; twice it was none.

Whoever updates a self-counting number measures after writing. The gap number of §4.5 counts its own documentation along — and, at second order, the explanation of its movement as well. One commit carried, at its own place, the value that had held before the explaining sentence. The wrong value still stands in the history, with hash and reason; the error is worth more than its removal.

If a measurement is repeated after a change, it runs first with the seed set of the previous move and must reproduce its numbers exactly. The other rules check a result against an expectation; this one checks whether the route measures the same thing in two moves.

Before a name is assigned, it is checked whether it is occupied — in the corpus or in its surroundings; if it is, the meaning decides and not the proximity. Two measured cases: one letter that denoted two different things in the same strand, and one name under which the corpus carries a different operation — with that one the two theorems of a whole module would have been trivial, at a green build.

A rule of this house, and ours: a number is not a map. The direction of an embedding may carry a role, an order of values may not; where the two coincide, the embedding counts. What it wards off, the report shows in O3: an identification of place-value index and truth value that Kaehr finds in Günther in 1981. That the number counts and orders no roles agrees with Kaehr. The sequence of contextural dichotomies is, he writes, "from the standpoint of the proemial relation internally not linearly but tabularly ordered", and: "Solely the determination of the number of contextures, or the m-valuedness of the logical system, proceeds by the linearity of the natural numbers." (our translations) (Kaehr 1981, PDF p. 10 (edition p. 9), read in the text layer). That is our assignment, for the direction of an embedding as carrier of a role does not occur in him.

5.2 The pitfalls

The pitfall file carries its entries; how many there are stands in docs/kennzahlen.md. Several concern not the proof language but the tool chain, and the newest are measurement errors in their most unpleasant shape:

An empty result gives pause; a slightly too small one does not. An unquoted path list on the command line delivered zero for every search word — noticeable because for every one. An output command, by contrast, prints a different wording for axiom-free theorems than for all others; whoever searches only for the one finds 415 instead of 487 names (measured at that state, c3b28cb, 5 August); axiom-free at that state were 72 guards. A closure from 415 seed names can be computed, no intermediate figure looks odd, and it was found solely by the calibration of set size against set size.

And a character class loses what it does not contain. Lean names may contain ? and !; a class built without them lost exactly one of 501 names — and a second route cut the same name at the same place. Two routes, the same blind spot.

An output breaks at the line length, not at the name length. For the most frequent profile: line length equals name length plus 61. Measured: at 116 no break, at 123 a break; the interval is open in its middle, because no measuring point lies between. A broken expectation text is the correct form and no sloppiness — it is not smoothed, because the comparison is verbatim. And whoever sets a series by a template breaks the short ones.

A range list older than the move measures the previous state and reports it as the new one. The result can be right and the route blind — no counter-probe that looks at the value shows this. The range list is therefore generated after the move; and a list generated before it is correct for the reproduction run and wrong for the new measurement.

Text extraction inserts hyphens at line breaks. Whoever checks verbatim quotations without removing them gets a false alarm at two of five correct places. What makes this dangerous is not the false alarm but its rate: a check that fires at two of five correct places will not be read the third time — and then it misses the one real deviation it was built for.

Searching for the mark word finds no pattern speech. The register of interpretation and posit marks was built from the marks that were known — and therefore failed to find one of the two constitutive posits of the aggregate, because its header states the status in the language of the construction pattern.

At every extension of a reference work the set test applies, not the comparison of numbers. A first attempt lost nine entries (mark register rev. 2, 4 August), and the grown number looked good.

5.3 The lint

By kind, not by number. The number of groups stood in three consecutive editions verbatim the same — "three" — and was twice overtaken by reality without any edition noticing; the third even called it "unchanged". Not a number that was updated wrongly, but one that was not updated at all and therefore stayed while it became false.

Reporting groups are read and do not enforce: the rank claims and the conceptual relapse. What they have in common is that judging them requires a judgement — a proximity window also hits benign cases, and a rank claim can be admissible. A breaking lint gets circumvented, a reporting one gets read.

Breaking groups set the exit code: the concept register; the unguarded printing of an axiom profile; the numerals of the carrier table outside edition and draft. What they have in common is that their finding carries no discretion and their baseline is zero — that is the condition under which a breaking lint is read rather than circumvented. Exceptions carry a justified mark and no tolerance of counts.

Which groups exist at the generation state, and how many, is said by the lint's own report — not by this paragraph.

A side use that costs nothing: an unchanged rank figure after a text move is at the same time the control that the new text carries no rank phrase. An existing instrument as a check for a move it was not built for.

5.5 What the fourth edition learnt methodically

The third edition explained its reticence by an asymmetric burden of proof: against overstatement there is an apparatus, against understatement nothing, so the text drifts downward. This explanation is half refuted. A review of the negations found not only statements that were too weak, but also one that was too strong — a "provably" in the closing picture where the carrier expressly says interpretation.

What explains both directions: a mark set by the carrier is lost on the way into the paper. From "carries no decidability instance" comes "is not decidable"; from "interpretation" comes "provably". And the loss does not hit negations only: the overlap [45] is a cardinality theorem in its carrier's header and stood in the paper as a construction prohibition.

Hence the obligation under which Part A is written, and it has three limbs: every negation carries a route as a rank claim does; every upgrade carries a measurement; and where the carrier sets a mark, the paper carries it along.

A further distinction has arisen in the process which the corpus did not have before. A negation claims either that something does not exist, or that this project does not build it, does not claim it, does not exclude it. The first is a statement about the world and needs checking; the second is one about the project's own self-binding and can be looked up in file headers. The sentence about the limit object looks like the first and is the second.

And one lesson of the fifth edition concerns the anchoring itself. A number-word about one's own apparatus — "three groups", "fifteen entries" — carries no anchor and therefore rots where an anchored figure would merely age. Two such sentences of the fourth edition were false, and that is why the fifth edition exists.

The Mathematical Shape of the Architecture — Part C: The Limit

About this part

This part brings together the measured limits of the build. A limit of which one knows what it hangs on is a result; none of the following is carried here as a defect.

C.1 · The proemial relation

What Günther claims. The proemial relation is the relation in which what connects can itself become what is connected at another level, and conversely. The exchange is "not mutual". It is "a mere potential" that becomes actual only as exchange or as order, and it "constitutes relation as such". It governs the connection of cognition and volition, heterarchically (source-verified, C&V pp. 19–21, 33).

What was attempted. The build has tried to carry the relation in several attempts over several phases. The attempts, their kinds and the reasons for their failure are in the carrier note (Part B).

Where it failed. From Günther's own determination a prefilter is derived: a carrier of the relation would have to be a fibre over what is connected (posited, derived from [T6]). About other fibres the prefilter does not decide. What the corpus carries on this is exactly: the two maps that forget in the corpus — the kenogrammatic descent and the projection of an arrow onto its source — are demonstrably not injective (descent_not_injective, arrow_left_not_injective) (proved [99], [97]). The collecting carrier ProemialCarrier distributes the features of the relation over its fields and remains a placement, not a carrier.

The limit of extension has no ground in Günther's text, but a direction. This shows in four steps.

First, Günther's distinction. Of the theory that concerns him he writes: "It is of utmost importance that this theory should not be confused with the description of the possible connection between a relation and a relatum. This can easily be taken care of by traditional logical devices. We shall concentrate from now on the trans-classic relation between relator and a relatum." (C&V p. 20, checked on the page image; the italics are Günther's).

Second, what for him is the traditional: "A two-valued logic (as far as it is relational at all) deals only with relations, meaning: with a pre-established synthesis between relationship (relator) and relatum." (p. 19, checked on the page image). That such a synthesis means fixed roles without exchange is our reading.

Third, the mathematics, without Günther. In the foundation of this construction a higher-order function sees of its argument only the table; there is more only at representations. That is proved [106], and it is textbook knowledge, not a theorem about Günther.

Fourth, the connection. Günther's text points in the same direction as this theorem: what traditional logic can achieve is the pre-established connection, and more than that a function does not see of its argument either. The theorem is not grounded by Günther's text. That the two belong together is our assignment. "Direction" is our word; it does not stand in Günther.

What is open. Several ways are open; the carrier note names them. Only the intensional one has a built substructure. Whether a fibre over relators fulfils the claim is decided neither by the prefilter nor by the source (programme).

What it is good for. Buridan shows it (Part A).

The relation in IGN. In Identität, Gegenidentität und Negativsprache the word proemial does not occur (measured, over the version of IGN). For cognition and volition IGN sets number as a neutral medium. This is neither a contradiction of C&V nor a revision. C&V denies a fixed dominance of the one over the other, IGN p. 53 a symmetrical exchangeability (source-verified). Both leave the same space that C&V gives the proemial relation: an exchange that is not mutual. The will's "transcendental prius" means, in IGN, underivability, not dominance (source-verified, IGN p. 53).

C.2 · The second negation as a definitional gap

A single negator is, in the corpus, a permutation of the values; its type alone keeps it within their range (definitional, by type). A definition that would lead a step out of the contexture as a single operation the corpus does not have; the item is open (L07-4) (programme). This is a gap of definition, not a limit of the subject matter: a build could overcome it. What exists is the order of the steps (Part A, second negation).

C.3 · The ass's choice

An animal that has to eat, and an environment that gives it no directive from which its will could take its orders. From this Günther reads off what a choice changes in the world: first an order, then an exchange that is neutral between subject and object, then the order the animal founds by choosing. → Buridan

Which haystack the animal eats, this paper leaves open, and it may not derive it: a theorem that derived the choice would reduce action to a ground, and with that there would be no freedom (Günther, following Schelling; source-verified, IGN p. 53; the application to the corpus is a reading).

This distinguishes this limit from the one Chapter 7 names as the third: that only the that of the act is no structural feature. There a calculus cannot; here it may not. The other limits in this part are findings; this one is a condition.

C.4 · AT-1b as the limit of semantics

For the step beyond the eighth place Günther names two conditions (source-verified, Lille p. 19). The first, that the step arises from the exhaustion of reflection, is built. The second, that the seven values move into the designative, is neither built nor claimed: the corpus carries no semantics of values, and its modules claim none. Whether the limit could be crossed is open.

Günther draws a limit of the same kind elsewhere. In IGN the criterion that distinguishes within the realm of number is language, and the languages are fixed to the values and to designation (source-verified, IGN pp. 53 f.). That this is the same limit that is called AT-1b here is an attribution over two steps (assignment). The corpus works in the layer of number and at its syntactic edge; above it, designation begins.

C.5 · The fifth limit

The previous edition left open in Chapter 7 whether the bound of the separation theorem belongs to the subject matter or to the frame. It belongs to the frame, and it hangs on one order axiom: the antisymmetry of ≤, from which the asymmetry of < follows (computed: complete enumeration outside the corpus; no theorem, no module). Without this signature one would read the statement at the level of the strict order; there it is the definition of asymmetry, turned around once, and the class of its counterexamples is empty.

The two horns of the separation theorem carry different marks. That the order horn is asymmetric is a synopsis with a derivation step that is not Günther's (synopsis). That the exchange horn is equivalence stands in Günther (source-verified, C&V p. 32). The localization explains the separation theorem; it does not derive it. The fourth limit of the previous edition is thereby determined at its place.

C.7 · What the report leaves open

Whether validity in the free system entails validity in the quotient, for admissible formulas and under the designation {1, 2}, is open; we call it conjecture V. The obvious proof by an invariant fails at partiality: no value of the quotient is read in every fibre, each is missing in exactly one; Günther's 3, for instance, has no reading in the fibre 1-2. At a connective over p and N₁p, a fibre that reads the result takes over the arbitrary value of an argument it does not read. No counter-example has been found. The search ran over all 64 admissible triples with N₁ and N₂, under the designation {1, 2} and the validity of this report: with one variable up to formulas of ten building blocks, with two variables up to six; a building block is a variable, a negator or a connective. The search ran over meanings: every formula is represented by the smallest one with the same effect in the free system and in the quotient (calculation of 3 October 2026, not built).

A generator that delivers for every finite-valued logic a calculus with proved soundness is, according to our search, not available machine-checked. The implementing instance searched on 3 October 2026 in the holdings of Lean (Mathlib, locally and in the file tree of the main branch, two further libraries and the repository search of GitHub), Isabelle (the Archive of Formal Proofs, the topics proof theory and paraconsistent logics), Coq and Rocq (opam, coq-community, GitHub), Agda, HOL4, HOL Light, ACL2 and Metamath; further in Crossref, OpenAlex and arXiv, in the repository of MUltlog, at vordenker and in the text layer of all files of our own corpus. The search on the web was carried out by three sub-agents of the same model family, commissioned by the implementing instance; what they found, the instance checked itself at the load-bearing findings, not at the others. Such a generator is a project, not a result of this report.

Of transjunctions, Günther's first case is built: his eight implications as transjunctions of the third grade, with the three grades and the total transjunction (the report, O5). Open are the transjunctions beyond that, and with them the transjunctional rules whose proof of correctness, as Kaehr writes in 2005, is still outstanding (O4). Open is evaluation with sharing (O1). What is shared is the same member that stands in two coupled applications at once, as one node instead of two copies. The sharing itself Mahler's model has: his combinator machine treats all occurrences of a variable "as instances of a single object" (our translation) (1993, PDF p. 235 (edition p. 223), checked on the page image). Open is the theorem that simultaneous evaluation of both at this node yields the same as any order. It too would not gain simultaneity; so we read Mahler's own diagnosis of his model: "The simultaneous evaluation of the proemially coupled processes is ensured by the scheduler mechanism." And: "This global analysis of the control flow shows clearly that the simultaneous change of function operator:operand observed from the point of view of the processes is only a secondary interpretation of the actual process." Even when shared, sameness is presupposed, not gained (O1): "The sameness of an object is defined in the proposed model by physical equality or pointer equality." (our translations) (all three 1993, PDF p. 235 (edition p. 223), checked on the page image). Open is the seam to Kaehr: how his calculus for the free system and a calculus for the quotient relate via the residue-class map. More than three values in Pfalzgraf and Kaehr are outstanding; on the side of the negations the corpus already has theorems about four values and about every value number.

C.8 · Regulative and constitutive — by what "operational" is to be measured

The report carries a word of Günther's in its title. Günther said himself what is technically repeatable for him, and he said it also of the circles that Part A and the report deal with. He says it first of the splitting of I and Thou, following McCulloch, whom he names at the beginning of the same paragraph: "But in the splitting of I and Thou we encounter a constitutive property of the world. And insofar as it is constitutive, it is also repeatable and thus accessible to technology." (our translation) (IGN p. 57; the first sentence checked on the page image, the second on the page image by two hands). Two paragraphs further on he thinks it proper "to apply Kant's separation of the two categories of concepts also to the heterarchical circles", and judges: "The Hamiltonian full cycles undoubtedly correspond to what in Kantian philosophy is called a regulative concept. At least from four-valuedness on – since in the triadic the difference between a circle at which not all, and another at which all permutations of the negative take part, does not yet exist. As far as worlds of richer structure are concerned, however, only partial cycles of negativity occur in them as logically constitutive – hence technically repeatable." (our translation) (p. 57, checked on the page image by two hands; the italics are Günther's). The equation "constitutive, hence repeatable" stands in Günther first for I and Thou; to the circles he transfers the distinction himself. Whether McCulloch uses the pair of concepts regulative and constitutive we have not checked; the text does not ascribe it to him.

"At least from four-valuedness on" belongs to the judgement on the full cycles. The three-valued circle, the hexagon of the report, Günther thereby expressly excepts. Affected are the four-valued full cycles of the corpus, among them Günther's 44. That Günther's "partial cycles" include the pseudo-circles of Table IX he does not say, and we do not connect the two places.

The corpus counts full cycles, for four values and for every value number. Whether they are constitutive in a world, it does not say. We read Günther's distinction such that it concerns the role of a circle in a world, not its occurrence in the calculus. That is our reading, and none of our theorems carries it. What this report calls "operational" must let itself be measured at this limit: proved, executable and checkable are the circles as objects of the calculus. That they would be technically repeatable in Günther's sense, the report does not claim.
